Denver vs. Seattle Homelessness

Inspired by this New York Times article.

The key insight: homeless people will move into a room with a door they can lock. Denver offered these rooms and enforced a camping ban, and street homelessness fell by two-thirds.

Since 2022, Denver has added about 940 shelter rooms with a door. Seattle and King County added none until this year.

Denver has an enforcement ladder. Seattle has a sweep, and it uses it six times as often with fewer police.

Camp sweeps per 100 people sleeping outside

Details

Seattle: 2,504 encampment removals in 2024 (city logs obtained by public records) for 4,585 people counted unsheltered that January, about 55 per 100. Most were same-day "obstruction" removals with no shelter offer required; of 1,884 referrals made during 2024 removals, 903 became a shelter stay. Denver: about 116 posted cleanups in 2022 (58 in the first half, per the city auditor) for 1,313 unsheltered, about 9 per 100, hatched because the full-year figure is an estimate. 2022 is the last year Denver's unit was consistent; from late 2023 it closed camps only once it had a room for everyone on the list, then kept the block closed.

Denver has an enforcement ladder

Every contact with someone camping climbs the same rungs, and each rung comes with an offer of a room.

  • Rung 1, the offer: a police officer and a social worker arrive together. The city's position is that a room is available; by its account no one has turned one down.
  • Rung 2, verbal warning: camping here is illegal under the 2012 ban; pack up. In 2023 police logged 297 such contacts.
  • Rung 3, written warning: three were issued in 2023.
  • Rung 4, ticket or arrest: 14 camping tickets in all of 2023, 4 in early 2024. Trespass enforcement, the real teeth, roughly doubled.
  • Then the block stays closed: 18 camps resolved with everyone placed, 350+ blocks closed to camping, and outreach returns whenever a tent reappears.

The threat is rarely used because it rarely has to be.

Seattle has one rung: the sweep

There is no camping ban, so there is nothing to warn about and no ticket to write. The only tool is removal, and removal leads nowhere.

  • No offer to make: about 85% of removals are filed as "obstructions," which require no notice and no shelter offer. When an offer is made it is usually a mat in a congregate hall from "a handful" of beds offered again and again.
  • No consequence for staying: the city attorney does not prosecute camping, and a 2023 court ruling found the obstruction rule unconstitutional (stayed on appeal).
  • So people come back: a UW study of Seattle's largest clearances found a displaced person was 14 times more likely to vanish from services than to enter shelter, and a year later was a median 717 meters from the cleared site.
  • So the same camps are swept again: 2,504 removals in 2024, about seven a day, and 2,017 tents cleared in the first five months of 2026, more than any year before.

With 1.2 officers per 1,000 residents and no ladder to climb, Seattle spends its enforcement on repetition.

The difference is focus. Denver's leaders chose rooms and a camping ban. Seattle's leaders argued about sweeps and tiny homes for five years.

Appendix

Seattle's strategic mistakes, in five bullets

  • It built nothing in the middle. From 2021 to 2025 Seattle added no net private-room shelter while its street population grew. The regional authority's "housing, not shelter" stance turned a real goal into a reason to refuse the one product unsheltered people reliably accept.
  • It ran the hotel rooms it bought as lifetime tenancies. King County purchased about 1,400 hotel rooms, the same move Denver made, then filled them as permanent supportive housing with near-zero turnover. The capital was spent; the throughput was given away.
  • It swept without a consequence or an offer. About 2,500 removals a year, 85% with no notice, backed by a congregate mat half of people decline and no camping ban to enforce. People move a few hundred meters and the same camps are cleared again. In 2025 Seattle budgeted more for this than for rooms with a door.
  • It never put one accountable office in charge with a number. Denver's mayor declared an emergency, set a target, bought buildings in weeks and ran a daily by-name command. Seattle delegated to an authority that stalled, let hotel rooms sit empty for 16 months, and is now being dismantled mid-crisis.
  • It confused spending with capacity. Seattle and King County spend roughly three times what Denver does on homelessness, so the failure was allocation and speed, not money.

Two caveats belong with this. Seattle's problem is genuinely larger per capita, partly for reasons policy cannot touch (climate, counting method), so Denver's result is not fully transferable. And Mayor Wilson has begun correcting the first mistake, slowly, with a build-only plan that could not meet her own target.

Seattle's hotel prices and labor run higher than Denver's, so add a third to the scaled figures to be safe.

Permanent supportive housing: what it is, and how the two cities use it

A PSH tenant gets a lease on a studio of roughly 300 to 400 square feet in a building with on-site staff and voluntary case management, with no time limit. Rent is 30% of income, about $290 a month for someone on SSI; the subsidy covers the rest. No equity, standard lease rules, eviction possible for lease violations but not for declining services. The "$500,000 unit" is the cost to build new in Seattle, not the value of what the tenant occupies; a hotel conversion costs far less (King County about $280,000 a room, Denver about $150,000), plus roughly $25,000 to $35,000 a year to operate. It is for chronically homeless people with a disability, ranked by vulnerability; King County has about 7,800 PSH beds against roughly 6,400 chronically homeless people outside, and waits run years. One-year retention is typically 85% to 90%, exits are mostly to another subsidized unit or death, and it reliably keeps its tenants housed and saves public money for the highest-cost users. It does not turn over, which is why it absorbs almost no one from the street in a given year.

Rapid rehousing, defined. A short-term rental subsidy paired with help finding an apartment: a lease in the person's own name on the private market, with the program paying most of the rent for six to twelve months (sometimes up to twenty-four) and a case manager helping with the search, deposit and first months. When the subsidy ends the person keeps the lease and pays full rent. It is cheap and fast, works best for people whose homelessness came from a financial shock, and worst for people with serious illness or addiction. Its failure mode is a return to homelessness when the subsidy stops.

Where people go when they leave Denver's rooms

Denver does not publish the split between lifetime subsidy and independence, and its auditor says the city cannot tell them apart well. Since July 2023 about 50% of people leaving Denver's shelters went to what the city calls stable housing, about 30% had negative exits (street, jail), and about 20% are unknown. The dashboard's "moved to housing" total (about 6,200) is a running count that never removes anyone who returns to homelessness, and it mixes permanent housing with transitional programs, self-paid hotels and temporary stays with family, which made up 13% of progress toward the 2025 goal. The exits are mostly rapid rehousing: the 2025 budget bought it for 250 households against 165 vouchers, PSH placements come only from turnover, and Urban Institute's staff interviews say "we are really leaning very, very heavily on rapid rehousing." By 2026, with federal money gone, HOST reports fewer vouchers, lost rapid-rehousing funding and average stays rising from 166 to 229 days. Whether rapid-rehousing households keep their leases when the subsidy ends is unknown; Denver has no one-year retention measure and the auditor has asked for one.

Why Mayor Wilson's shelter promise stalled

She promised a thousand new beds a year. By mid-September 2026 she had opened 187 (Olympic Hills 45, Belmont 31, Brighton 14, Bayside 75, a youth site 24) and reset the goal to 960 over 2026 and 2027. Five reasons, in rough order of weight: she chose build over buy or rent, so each site needs land, a lease, a permit, a neighbor fight and an operator, six to twelve months apiece, where Denver's rooms existed on day one; her money is locked by a $175M deficit and a council proviso requiring the $30M sweeps team; the regional authority collapsed under her, pulling a $3M village grant in February and ending with a $44.7M negative cash finding; siting politics, with the village size cap only raised this year; and the one-time federal money that built Denver's program was spent in Seattle in 2021–22 on hotel rooms that closed. The missing ingredient is not appropriations but the willingness to short-circuit process: declare the emergency, buy the building, absorb the neighborhood fights at once, and turn the sweeps budget into a rooms budget. Johnston did all four in his first six months.

A note on Portland

Portland's Mayor Keith Wilson hit his target of 1,500 new shelter beds by December 2025, and Multnomah County's unsheltered count still rose by roughly 1,600 over the following year. The congregate model is part of the explanation, but only part: the beds are overnight-only (8 p.m. to 6 a.m.), so people are back on the street each morning; nothing sits behind them, with congregate shelters moving 12% of people to housing against a 41% goal and occupancy running 20% to 70%; and the money was one-time, so the two largest shelters have already closed. Denver's rooms run full because they are 24-hour, come with a case manager, and are paired with a closed block. Portland built the wrong product and paired it with nothing.

How the 3.6× gap splits
¼ + ¾
Roughly a quarter is more homelessness; three quarters is the unsheltered share. So the explanations that matter most are about shelter, weather, and what a sweep comes with.

1. Seattle's shelters are full every night. Denver's can flex. Strong evidence

King County has about one emergency bed for every three homeless people, shelters run above 93% full, and an average of 3% of Seattle's ~5,000 beds were free on a given night in 2024. The beds offered during a sweep are, in one reporter's words, "a handful, and they offer those beds again and again." Seattle's sheltered count is capped by beds, not demand.

Denver's count area reported 4,943 overflow beds in 2024 against King County's 188. In the January 2026 cold snap Denver had room for about 700 people in cold-weather shelters and more than 600 came. When Denver closes a camp, there is somewhere to put everyone.

Details

Beds per homeless person in the count area: King County 0.36; Metro Denver 1.18 (2024), 0.77 (2025). City level, 2024: Seattle Metro 5,003 beds for 9,440 people (0.53); Denver sheltered 5,266 of 6,539 (at least 0.81).

2. Denver counts on freezing nights. Seattle counts in the mild. Strong evidence

Denver's count night was −6°F in 2026 and below freezing in 2025, with cold-weather shelters open. Seattle's January is around 42°F and its survey runs over two weeks. In a national study of 25,000 homeless adults, 35% were unsheltered where January averages below 25°F versus 66% where it averages 45°F or more.

This cuts in Denver's favor on the one night that gets measured, but it only works because Denver has the beds to open. Denver's 2024 count, used for the 3.6× figure, was a warm night.

3. The two counts are different instruments. This shrinks the gap, not closes it. Moderate

King County's sampling method, used since 2022, finds people a 4 a.m. street tally misses. UW's own estimate is that it added about 13% over what the old method would have shown in 2022. Denver's volunteer tally is widely considered a serious undercount; a former head of the count agency once called it "a gross undercount, probably by half."

Adjusting both (Seattle down ~13%, Denver up 25–50%) takes the 2024 ratio from 3.6× to roughly 2.1–2.5×. A real gap remains. Vehicle residents, often raised as a Seattle quirk, are about 27% of King County's unsheltered; even with none of them, Seattle's non-vehicle unsheltered would be 2.6× Denver's total.

4. After a Seattle sweep, people vanish. After a Denver resolution, the block stays closed. Moderate

A University of Washington study of Seattle's twelve largest clearances found that a person displaced by a sweep was 14 times more likely to disappear from the service system than to enter shelter, and that a year later the median person was still within 717 meters of the cleared site. In 2024 only 48% of the city's 1,884 sweep "referrals" turned into a shelter stay.

Denver resolves a camp only once it has a room for everyone on the by-name list, then keeps the block closed under its 2012 camping ban and re-contacts anyone who returns. Washington also had no drug-possession crime from the 2021 Blake ruling until July 2023; 41% of King County's unsheltered reported a substance-use disorder in 2026, against 17% of the sheltered. That effect is plausible but unmeasured.

Bottom line

Seattle has about 1.45 times Denver's homelessness per resident, a housing story. But three quarters of its unsheltered gap comes from a much higher share of homeless people sleeping outside, and that share is set by three things that reinforce each other: a shelter system with no slack, mild winters that never force the question, and sweeps that move people a few hundred meters instead of into a room. Measurement differences shrink the gap to perhaps 2 to 2.5 times. They do not erase it.

What the numbers cannot tell you

The counts are not the same instrument

Denver's count is a volunteer street census every January. Seattle's is a slice of a countywide count that since 2022 is a statistical estimate from a sample survey, done every other year, and the city slice is published only some years. The 2024 county total was revised upward twice after release. Treat the Seattle line as a trend, not a census.

Chronic-unsheltered is inferred

Neither city publishes a count of chronically homeless people sleeping outside within city limits. Both figures here apply the chronic share reported for the wider count area (Metro Denver, King County) to the city's unsheltered count. They are marked as estimates on the charts.

Weather and migrants

Denver's 2026 count fell on a freezing night with emergency shelters open. HUD's 2024 count for Metro Denver included about 4,300 migrants in city-funded hotels, which is why HUD's total that year is far above the local report; this page uses the local figures.

"Sweep" means different things

Seattle logs each same-day removal of an obstruction as one event. Denver's countable events were 7-day-posted cleanups through 2022 and multi-day "resolutions" that placed a whole camp into hotel rooms in 2023–24; the city keeps no central log, its auditor has twice said so, and routine move-alongs are untracked in both cities. Denver's sweep chart is mostly estimates for that reason; its enforcement lives in the warnings-to-tickets ladder and the rising trespass numbers.

Money

Denver's program cost at least $178M, heavily from expired federal pandemic funds, and is now shrinking. Seattle spends more on shelter overall (about 5,000 city-funded beds, 2,850 of them units) but most of it is congregate. The question the data cannot settle is whether Denver can hold its gains on a city budget.
