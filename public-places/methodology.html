People sleeping outside

About 5,600 people slept outside within Seattle's city limits on a night in late January 2026: likely 5,400 to 5,750, up about 24% from about 4,500 in January 2024. The number will change when King County publishes its 2026 Seattle figure.

What we count

Everyone sleeping outside on one night: in a tent, on the street or in a vehicle.

This is what the federal count calls unsheltered: anyone whose bed that night is a place not meant for sleeping.

Counted

  • Tents and makeshift shelters, anywhere
  • Sidewalks, doorways, parks, under bridges
  • Cars, vans, trucks, RVs and boats
  • Abandoned buildings and other places not meant for sleeping

Not counted

  • Shelters, including tiny home villages and hotel shelter rooms
  • Transitional housing
  • Jail, hospital or treatment
  • Staying on a friend's couch

Why we don't use "chronically homeless"

The federal definition of chronic homelessness requires a year or more without a home plus a disability, both self-reported in a survey. In King County it swung from 66% of people outside in 2024 to 38% in 2026, far more than the population could have changed in two years.

What "Seattle" means in the county's numbers

The county reports a "Seattle Metro" area, not the city. It's slightly bigger.

The King County Regional Homelessness Authority draws subregions around where people go for services, not city lines. From its 2024 report [4]:

  • Seattle Metro includes Vashon-Maury Island "because most unsheltered people report receiving services in Seattle."
  • White Center and Skyway, unincorporated areas next to the city, "were considered continuous with Seattle Metro."
  • People are placed by where they were surveyed, not where they slept. Someone sleeping in Seattle who took the survey in Burien counts toward South King County.

We take Vashon out of Seattle Metro. White Center, Skyway and people surveyed across the city line are left in, because no source says how many they are.

How we got 5,600

King County hasn't published its 2026 Seattle figure yet, so we take Seattle's share from a University of Washington report and subtract Vashon.

  1. The county count. The January 2026 count found 11,829 people outside countywide. The initial report has no subregion breakdown. [1]
  2. Seattle Metro's vehicle residents. The University of Washington team that runs the count reported 1,315 people living in vehicles in Seattle Metro, 23% of its unsheltered population. [2]
  3. Divide. That puts Seattle Metro at about 5,700. The 23% is rounded, so the range is 5,600 to 5,850.
    1,315 ÷ 0.23 = 5,717
  4. Take out Vashon. The 2024 count found 90 homeless people on Vashon; a local organizer counted 149, possibly near 200. [5] We subtract about 100.
    5,717 − ~100 ≈ 5,600 within the city (range 5,400 to 5,750)

What this number doesn't tell you
  • It's one night. The UW team estimates 1.33 different people over a year for each vehicle resident on the count night. Applied to everyone, that's about 7,400 people a year within the city. [2]
  • It's a survey estimate. Its margin of error is wider than the range above.
The trend and who is outside

The King County totals for 2022, 2024 and 2026 are from the county's counts [3] [4], and Seattle's 2024 figure is Seattle Metro's 4,585 less Vashon [5]. Counts since 2022 use the same survey method; earlier counts aren't comparable.

Substance use disorder (41%), serious mental illness (31%) and chronic homelessness (38%) are the county's 2026 survey of people outside [1], applied to Seattle's 5,600. They're self-reported, so they likely undercount. Seattle probably runs higher: 54% of Seattle's vehicle residents are chronically homeless, against 43% countywide [2].

    The 2015 State of Emergency

    On November 2, 2015, Seattle Mayor Ed Murray proclaimed a civil emergency on homelessness, and King County Executive Dow Constantine declared one for the county the same day. City Council approved the proclamation unanimously the next day. Murray said the emergency would give the city more flexibility in contracting for services and distributing resources, which is what a 12-month build-out needs. See KUOW, November 2, 2015.

    Who accepts a hotel room

    The pledge says that at one Seattle camp, 73% accepted a hotel room offer. That's JustCARE's 2020–21 outreach at the encampment on South King Street: of 78 people there, 57 moved into hotel rooms. See KUOW, August 25, 2021. It's one camp, with intensive outreach, so it's not a citywide rate.

    Why people turn down shelter

    The pledge says the top reason people gave for turning down a shelter bed in Seattle was "wanted a tiny home." That's from the Seattle Human Services Department and Unified Care Team's quarterly report to City Council for July to September 2023, Table 3. Of 1,243 people who turned down a shelter offer and gave a reason, 23% said they wanted a tiny house. It was also the top reason in April to June 2023 (16%).
