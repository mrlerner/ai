How the simulator works, and what's estimated

People outside. About 5,600 people slept outside within Seattle's city limits in January 2026 (how we count). The simulator spreads them across the places that got encampment reports in September 2026, in proportion to the reports. Reports aren't a census, so locations are rough.

The mayor's plan. 960 tiny homes with full 24/7 services. With building costs spread over five years, that's about $35 million a year, the budget for every plan. Her villages take about seven months to site and open, and the city starts about one a month, which matches her real pace: about 280 places in her first ten months.

Clearing public places. Outreach works through your public places in the order you ranked them. Everyone there gets an offer. People who accept move in; people who say no must leave the protected place. It counts as cleared once everyone in it has had an offer, so it clears only as fast as places open.

Who needs what. Of every 20 people, the simulator assumes 9 mostly need a place, 5 need some support (often addiction) and 6 need a lot (often serious mental illness). In the 2026 county count, 31% of people outside reported a serious mental illness and 41% an addiction. High-need people get full-support places first, then whatever is open.

Incidents. Calls for help, disturbances and complaints. A site has more when its residents need more support than it gives, and tent camps have a few more than buildings. Each is weighted by who's nearby: low on industrial land, higher on a residential block, and a bit higher for sites placed by decree. The simulator compares incidents at your sites with what the same people caused outside. Every plan includes naloxone, a daily check and someone who can respond.

Approval and headlines. Simulated, not a poll. Five groups of voters each care about different results, and the overall number is their weighted average. The outlets and stories are invented.
