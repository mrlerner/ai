The three cities

Denver: a room with a door, and a ladder

Camping ban since 2012. Mayor Johnston declared an emergency in July 2023.

What it did
Bought three hotels, leased two and built five villages of pallet units: about 1,284 private rooms in 16 months. It resolved camps only once it had a room for everyone on a by-name list, then kept the block closed. Each contact climbs a ladder: an outreach worker and an officer offer a room, then a spoken warning, a written warning, and only then a ticket. It wrote 14 camping tickets in all of 2023.
What it cost
About $45,000 per room per year to run, and at least $178M in all by the city auditor's count, much of it one-time federal money that has now run out.
What it got
Street homelessness fell 61% from 2022 to 2026, and encampment complaints fell 40%. About 18% of the roughly 3,800 people served had reached permanent housing by April 2025.
Take
The offer people accept (a room they can lock), the ladder, keeping cleared blocks closed, and one office in charge with a public number.
Watch out for
Funding that disappears. The leased hotels closed in 2025 and 2026, and the 2026 budget cut $12.4M from shelter. Denver also can't say how many people stay housed.

San Francisco: enforcement and treatment

Old laws against camping, held back by a court injunction until the 2024 Grants Pass decision. Mayor Lurie's "Breaking the Cycle" plan since 2025.

What it did
Cleared camps often once the courts allowed it, including on state highway land after a 2025 deal with Caltrans. Under a fentanyl emergency ordinance it opened treatment and sober beds quickly. It also opened a 16-bed unit at 822 Geary where police can drop someone off in about two minutes instead of six hours, and a $14.5M RESET center that takes people police arrest for public intoxication. Outreach workers stopped handing out drug-smoking supplies without a link to treatment. A 2025 law limits lived-in RVs, with permits, buybacks and a shelter that takes RV residents.
What it cost
We found no per-bed figure. It opened 863 shelter beds in 16 months but closed others, for a net gain of 403. At least 470 of the beds require sobriety or focus on addiction treatment.
What it got
Street homelessness fell 22% from 2024 to 2026, and people living in tents fell 85%. A record 57% of homeless people were sheltered on count night. Lived-in RVs fell more than 42%. Advocates say the new morning count and the sweeps overstate the drop. A civil grand jury found that only 30–40% of people in some city programs ended the year in stable housing.
Take
The police drop-off unit, the RV ladder, opening treatment the same day, and emergency powers to skip slow contracting.
Watch out for
Sweeps without enough rooms. Police call it the "forever sweep." Fewer tents can mean people lost their gear rather than came inside. In 2024 only 30% of shelter offers were accepted.

San Diego: a ban and a staffed tent camp

Unsafe Camping Ordinance, effective July 2023. Two "Safe Sleeping" sites on the edge of Balboa Park.

What it did
Banned camping at all times near schools and shelters, on transit, and in parks, canyons and riverbanks. Elsewhere camping is banned only when shelter is available, and police can't ticket at night. It opened about 760 tents on two fenced dirt lots in about 16 months. Each tent sleeps two and is open at the bottom, and staff are on site 24/7. Entry is by referral only, and the sites are low barrier: pets and partners are welcome, and no sobriety is required.
What it cost
About $9,000–11,000 per tent per year, from the council's contract figures. Each tent added in the 2024 expansion cost about $5,000 a year.
What it got
Downtown cleared, and about 1,000 people lived at the sites in 2024. But people sleeping outside citywide didn't go down, and encampment reports rose 13%. About 10% of people who left went to permanent housing, against 18% from the city's indoor shelters. 21 residents have died since 2023, and residents sued over the conditions.
Take
The law's always-banned zones around schools, transit and parks, and a staffed lot as a fast, cheap bridge.
Watch out for
A tent as the final offer. It fills up but rarely leads to housing, and a full camp switches off the citywide ban.

What all three have in common

  • A camping ban that is enforced. Seattle has sweeps, but no ban behind them.
  • Somewhere to send people. Each opened hundreds of places to sleep within about a year and a half, using emergency powers and buildings or land the city could get fast.
  • An outreach worker comes before the police. Tickets and arrests are rare compared with warnings.
  • Sidewalks cleared faster than homelessness fell. How many people moved into housing is the weak or missing number in all three.

Where they differ

  • What the city offers. Denver a locking room, San Francisco more and more a treatment or sober bed, San Diego a tent. This is the biggest difference, and it tracks the results.
  • How hard the city enforces. Denver climbs a ladder and holds the block, San Francisco clears often, San Diego mostly warns.
  • Cost per bed. About $10,000 a year for a San Diego tent, $45,000 for a Denver room. The cheaper offer housed the fewest people.
  • Drugs. Denver and San Diego are low barrier. San Francisco is adding sober beds.

What a tiny home costs to run, city by city

Seattle runs a tiny home for about $35,000 a year, near the bottom of the West Coast cities that publish a figure. City averages for staffed villages run $33,000–51,000 a home, because they all keep staff on site around the clock. The villages that cost far less are run by their residents.

  • Seattle is not overpaying compared with other cities. It runs a tiny home for about two-thirds of what Portland, San Francisco and Los Angeles spend.
  • Everyone pays for the same thing: staff on site 24 hours a day. A few small sites run cheaper, like Portland's $21,000 women's village, but no city's average is much below $33,000. The savings in pledge item 3 come from changing that staffing model, not from catching up with a cheaper city.
  • Not every city uses tiny homes. San Diego uses its staffed tent sites (about $10,000 a tent) and indoor shelters. Houston puts people straight into apartments. Austin's Esperanza Community is a state-sanctioned camp of shelters, with no published cost per unit.

The figures aren't measured the same way. Portland's are full actual costs, Denver's covers only the operator contract, and San Francisco's is an estimate made before opening. Los Angeles and Vancouver cabins usually sleep two, while most of Seattle's sleep one, so we compare per home, not per bed. San José's figures are from 2022–23.

The better strategy for Seattle

Take Denver's offer and ladder, San Diego's always-banned zones and San Francisco's tools for drugs and vehicles:

  1. Write the places into law, banned at all times. In San Diego, a ban that applies only when shelter is open switches off whenever the shelter fills.
  2. Make the offer a room with a door that locks. About 18% of Denver's people reached permanent housing, against about 10% from San Diego's tents.
  3. Use a staffed lot only as a bridge, to clear a school block while rooms open.
  4. Publish an enforcement ladder with a clear path to housing, recovery, or court, and hold the block. People come back unless the block stays closed and outreach returns.
  5. Borrow San Francisco's police drop-off unit and RV ladder. Vehicles are about a quarter of Seattle's unsheltered people.
  6. Report what the others didn't: deaths, returns to the street and a one-year housing check, every month.
