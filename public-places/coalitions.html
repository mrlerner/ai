Who could give the pledge weight

A pledge only changes things if the people behind it are ones a council member can't afford to ignore. This page asks who those people could be. Numbered notes are at the bottom.

The short version

  1. The status quo isn't an accident. It's where every organized interest's pushing balances out. Tents on a canal cost a council member less than a tiny home village in an organized neighborhood, or a press conference by advocates. Outrage alone doesn't change that.
  2. The other side wins with intensity, not numbers. The council passed an RV permit program with a veto-proof majority. Then a few advocacy groups, one council member and, by one count, about 600 emails got the mayor to veto it.
  3. Organizations, not signatures, carry weight. Each brings its members, a relationship with a council member, and its reputation. The hard work is months of one-on-one meetings and a shared ask they write together.
  4. Five groups are organized, have something politicians need, and have their own reasons to care: workers in public spaces, parents, the neighborhoods that already host shelters, congregations, and downtown institutions working alongside hotel workers.
  5. What they share is narrower than the pledge: no open drug use where kids walk and people work, with a room offered first. Camping in general splits them.

Why signatures aren't enough

A council member weighs three things when public places fill up:

  • A camp on the canal costs a little goodwill, and nine council members, the mayor and the county share the blame.
  • A village in the district creates organized neighbors who are angry at that council member by name.
  • Enforcement brings a press conference, and maybe a lawsuit, from groups that show up every time.

Clear public spaces help almost everyone a little. Clearing them costs a few organized groups a lot. So the few fight, and the many don't. A citywide list of people who agree doesn't change any of those three costs.

What moved the mayor on RVs. The council passed the permit program 6–3, with Councilmembers Rinck, Foster and Lin voting no. Then the Seattle/King County Coalition on Homelessness stood with the mayor at her press conference, the ACLU of Washington praised the veto, and Councilmember Rinck urged it. The coalition's director said almost 600 constituents emailed the council and the mayor. Of 63 people who signed up to speak at the vote, most were opposed. Councilmember Saka, who voted yes, now backs the mayor's alternative.

A few hundred people and a handful of organizations showed up at the moment of decision, in the right offices, as groups that will be back next time. That's the kind of force the pledge needs.

The tax pledge Republican candidates sign is the wrong model. It pinned down a position Republicans had held for decades. Homelessness splits Democrats, so a Seattle pledge picks a side in a live fight. If only people who already agree sign it, it sorts voters instead of moving anyone. Compassion Seattle had the same core idea as this pledge, and backing from the Downtown Seattle Association. It qualified with 34,714 signatures, and a court took it off the ballot.

The hard work of coalition building

  1. Organizations sign, not individuals. “72 neighborhood groups are asking you to sign this” is power. “10,000 people signed” isn't. Each organization brings members who vote, a relationship with a council member, and a reputation it is putting on the line.
  2. The wording is negotiated. Advocates' letters to the mayor are negotiations: what it says, who signs, who won't. Our pledge works the other way round: we wrote it, and now we ask people to sign. A statement several groups write together reads weaker but carries far more weight, because every word cost someone something.
  3. It starts with one-on-one meetings. Dozens of them, with leaders of groups that already exist. The point is to learn what they need, not to convince them of what we need: a problem they already have that this would solve.
  4. Agree on a solution, not just the problem. Pressure without a workable answer pushes politicians into a dead end, and they'll resist it.
  5. Hold together under pressure. On launch day, partners will get calls saying they're helping sweeps. They stay only if the ask is their own.
  6. Plan a moment. A room full of members, where candidates say yes or no to the ask, in public, before an election.

None of this can be done with a website or a map. The data helps prepare each meeting. The work itself takes months, people and relationships.

Five coalitions that could carry weight

We looked for groups that meet four tests: they're already organized; they have something politicians need, such as votes, endorsements, volunteers or standing; they're not already in the homelessness fight; and they have their own reason to care, so no one can call them selfish.

1. Workers in public spaces

Bus drivers, grocery and pharmacy clerks, librarians, park staff, janitors and hotel workers spend their shifts where the problem is. Some already have a live fight:

  • ATU Local 587, which represents King County Metro and Sound Transit workers, has asked for protection from people smoking fentanyl on buses: more security and a way to report drug use. Its president says drivers are reporting more of it. Drivers have asked the King County Board of Health to withdraw its statement that secondhand fentanyl smoke poses “no real risk.”
  • UFCW 3000 has organized members for safety at the U District Safeway.
  • Still to ask: library and parks staff, building janitors, downtown hotel and restaurant workers (UNITE HERE Local 8), and Harborview's nurses.

Union endorsements and volunteers count for more in Seattle elections than almost anything else. These unions sit in the same progressive coalition as the advocates. That changes the story from moderates against progressives to workers against a policy that fails workers. Start with locals that already have a grievance, not the citywide labor council.

2. Parents

In 2024, All Together for Seattle Schools brought out hundreds of parents against the district's school closure plan, and the district pulled back from it. Before that, no citywide parent network like it existed. Parents vote in high numbers, live in every district, and nobody can call them selfish. Schools is also the strongest part of the pledge: about 110 places near schools and child care have encampment reports.

The hard part: parent groups are focused on school funding, and PTAs stay out of elections. The ask has to be about children specifically, such as walking routes and drug use near schools, and come with rooms.

3. The neighborhoods that already host

In 2022, Chinatown-International District residents marched on City Hall, and King County dropped its plan to expand the SODO shelter next to the neighborhood. That is the most effective neighborhood organizing on this issue in recent years. It was used to block a shelter.

One neighborhood is too small to move the city. Together, though, the neighborhoods that already host shelters and carry open drug markets could make a progressive argument no one can call selfish: fair share. Those are Chinatown-ID and Little Saigon, Pioneer Square, Third Avenue, Aurora and Lake City, and South Park. “We've hosted. Now other neighborhoods take their share, and our streets get cleared.” That gives them a reason to want villages in wealthier districts, which goes straight at the siting fights that keep camps on the canal.

The risk: the 2022 protests drew a mix of people, including some who oppose shelters anywhere. The ask has to be fair share, not no shelters.

4. Congregations that build and host

Sound Alliance, a Puget Sound alliance of faith, labor and education groups, belongs to the Industrial Areas Foundation. That national network's method is the hard work described above: organizations as members, one-on-one meetings, and public meetings where officials say yes or no. State law lets religious organizations host people experiencing homelessness on their own property. Volunteers can build a tiny home in a day.

This coalition wouldn't demand rooms from the city. It would provide them, on its own land, with volunteers, and the siting fight goes away. In return, the city clears the school and park zones nearby and pays for case managers. It is the least head-on of the five. It is also slow, and each site holds dozens of homes, not hundreds.

5. Downtown institutions, with hotel workers

Benaroya Hall, Pike Place Market, the art museum, the sports teams and the convention center have money and civic weight. On their own they look like business, and Compassion Seattle showed how easily a business-led effort becomes the target. Standing with the hotel and restaurant workers whose jobs depend on downtown, it's about jobs.

Where they overlap

These groups don't agree about tents. Some of their members side with the advocates on camping and on sweeps. They are much closer on open drug use where kids walk and people work: at schools, at bus stops, on buses, outside stores and libraries. Using drugs in public has been a crime again under state law since 2023.

So a pledge written with them would probably be narrower than ours: drug use first, schools and transit first, with a room offered before anyone is moved. That's a smaller ask with far more weight behind it.

Who we're not counting on

  • Small businesses on their own. They're angry but stretched thin, and closing.
  • Business as the lead voice. Compassion Seattle and the 2019 council races made that an easy attack line. Business can support, not lead.
  • Urbanists and disability rights groups. Clear sidewalks and parks fit their values, but in Seattle they've mostly sided with the advocates.
  • Single-neighborhood groups, which are as likely to fight a village as to back one.

First conversations

Ten to fifteen meetings over a month, asking questions rather than pitching. What's happening where you work or live? What have you tried? What would you need to see? Who else should we talk to?

  • ATU Local 587 leaders, and a UFCW 3000 shop steward
  • All Together for Seattle Schools, and PTA presidents at schools with encampment reports nearby
  • Chinatown-International District community leaders
  • A Sound Alliance organizer, and two pastors whose congregations might host
  • UNITE HERE Local 8

Our data can make each meeting specific: the bus routes with the most drug calls, or the reports near one PTA's own school. Then the ask gets written with whoever is still at the table.
