Organizing timeline to the 2027 election

All seven district council seats are on the ballot in 2027. The goal is a list of verified voters in every district that's large enough for candidates to notice, ready before filing week, with the pledge as the one question every candidate has to answer. Signer goals below are suggestions to size the work.

  • Nov 2026 – Jan 2027
    Start    Set up the organization in the right legal form first (see what to check). Launch at Cal Anderson and one or two public places per district, each with its own sign and page. Publish the first monthly progress report from the city's report data, so the scoreboard exists before any politician does. Recruit a captain for each place: a neighbor who keeps the sign up and brings in signers. Goal: 1,000 signers.
  • Feb – Apr 2027
    Build    Signs go up at schools and parks in all seven districts. Build a coalition that crosses Seattle's usual split: PTSAs and parents near schools, park friends groups, community councils, neighborhood business groups, and at least one shelter or treatment provider who backs rooms first. Pitch reporters a data story every month ("188 places near parks and schools in District 3"). Invite sitting councilmembers to sign, especially those running again. Talk to people thinking about running. Goal: 500 verified signers per district by filing week.
  • May 2027
    Ask    As soon as filing week closes, send the pledge to every filed candidate, with a three-week deadline. Announce the dates of the candidate forums (below) at the same time.
  • June 2027
    Publish    Publish the five-item grid for every district, with "no answer" shown as plainly as yes or no. Hold a candidate forum on public places in each district, or a few combined forums. Captains door-knock and table near their places with the grid.
  • Mid-July 2027
    Ballots out    Primary ballots are mailed. Every signer gets their own district's grid by email and text, plus a reminder the week before the deadline. Goal: 2,000 verified signers per district.
  • August 3, 2027
    Primary    The top two in each district advance.
  • Aug – Sept 2027
    Narrow    Ask the finalists again; with only two left, a holdout stands out. Hold a second round of forums with just the two finalists, which is closer to a real debate.
  • Mid-October 2027
    Ballots out    General ballots are mailed. Send the final grid to every signer, with clips from the forums.
  • November 2, 2027
    General    Election day.
  • 2028 on
    Hold them to it    Grade the winners on their votes: the ordinance, the budget each fall, siting rules. The monthly encampment number is the scoreboard. The same list and pledge carry over to the 2029 city attorney and mayor races.

Candidate forums on public places

Private citizens can host candidate forums, and many Seattle neighborhood groups already do. A forum on public places gives every candidate the same questions in public, on the record, with video. Clips of a candidate dodging a yes-or-no on parks travel further than any petition. To make it work:

  • Invite every candidate in the race, on the same terms, and say who was invited. An empty chair with a name card is news. Inviting only friendly candidates turns a forum into a campaign event, which raises legal problems.
  • Co-host across the split so candidates can't write it off as one side's event: for example, a PTSA, a neighborhood business group and a shelter or treatment provider. A local newsroom or KUOW as co-host or moderator makes candidates likelier to come.
  • Use a neutral moderator, and publish the rules and question topics in advance. Opening statements, the same questions for each candidate with equal time, and a lightning round of yes-or-no questions taken from the five pledge items.
  • Ground the questions in the place. For example: "Your district had 188 places with encampment reports near parks and schools in September. What's that number on election day if you win?" Questions can also come from neighbors through the petition page.
  • Hold it at a public place on the list (a school gym, a library, a community center near the park), record it, and post the full video and short clips.
  • Check the rules first. If the host is a 501(c)(3), the IRS expects a forum to be nonpartisan and to cover a range of issues, which a single-topic forum may not meet. A 501(c)(4) or an informal neighborhood group has more room. Ask Washington's Public Disclosure Commission whether hosting costs count as contributions.
