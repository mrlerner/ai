First principles: what a room with a door does

Denver cut the number of people sleeping outside by 61% from 2022 to 2026, and San Francisco cut its number by 22% from 2024 to 2026 (January point-in-time counts). No city publishes acceptance rates by type of shelter, but program data puts ranges on four questions.

Start here: how many people are sleeping outside in Seattle?

About 5,600 within the city limits in January 2026. Method and sources: How Many People Sleep Outside in Seattle?

  • How it's derived. KCRHA hasn't published its 2026 Seattle figure. A University of Washington report on the same count gives 1,315 people living in vehicles in KCRHA's "Seattle Metro" subregion and says they are 23% of its unsheltered population, which gives about 5,700. Seattle Metro also includes Vashon Island (about 100 people), so the city limits hold about 5,600 (range 5,400–5,750).
  • It includes people living in cars and RVs. About 23% of Seattle's people outside lived in vehicles in 2026, about 1,300. About 4,300 were in tents, doorways and parks.
  • It's modeled from a survey sample, so it can't be compared with the pre-2022 street counts (about 3,700 in Seattle in 2020).
  • It's up about 24% from 2024, when KCRHA's count gave Seattle Metro 4,585, about 4,500 within the city.

1. Offer every chronically unsheltered person a room. How many stay outside?

About 10–30% turn down even a room. Group shelter is turned down far more often.

Why people say no in Seattle (Q3 2023, people who gave a reason): wanted a tiny house instead, 23%; didn't want the shelter offered, 15%; wouldn't go without a partner, family or friends, 11%. Every "wants a tiny house" answer came from someone offered only group shelter. In Seattle that leaves roughly 210–1,110 people who would refuse even a room estimate: 10–30% of the roughly 2,100–3,700 chronically unsheltered people.

2. How often does a room lead somewhere?

About one person in four or five reaches permanent housing within about a year, and most of them stay housed. About a third go back outside.

Program reports often look better because they divide by the people who left, not the people served. Seattle's city-funded tiny houses reported 46% of exits to permanent housing in 2024, and Portland's motel shelters 38%. Once housed, about 80% are still housed a year or two later in the Housing First trials (New York, 2004; Canada's At Home/Chez Soi). A successful exit, housed and still housed a year later, happens for about 15–30% of people who take a room estimate.

3. How much does the door matter compared with a group shelter?

A lot for getting people inside. Much less once they're in.

  • Getting inside: roughly 73–95% take a room against 25–46% who agree to a group-shelter referral, and fewer actually arrive. Couples, people with pets and people who've been robbed in shelters are the ones a door brings in.
  • Exits: rooms do better per person. In Seattle in 2019, villages sent 34–37% of the people who left to permanent housing, enhanced shelter 23% and basic mats 3–6%. In Portland in 2025: motels 38%, congregate 12%. In King County's 2020 hotel study, 60% of hotel leavers went to housing, against 13% from basic shelter.
  • Per dollar, the gap closes. Portland's motels produced 0.52 housing exits per bed per year and its congregate shelters 0.45, at $63k against $37k per bed. Counted that way, congregate yields more housing per dollar, but only for people willing to sleep there.

4. Can you put someone with a fentanyl addiction in a room with a lock?

Yes, and you have to: 41% of King County's unsheltered people reported a substance use disorder in 2026. But behind a locked door is where people die of overdoses.

  • Using alone is the risk. San Francisco residents of single-room hotels died of overdoses at 19 times the rate of other residents (2010–17). In US hotel and motel overdose deaths, someone who could have helped was present only 35% of the time (CDC, 2022–24). In New York, 56% of opioid deaths among homeless people happened in supportive housing or shelters, against 16% in public spaces.
  • Housing alone raised overdose deaths in the largest test: veterans given housing vouchers had about twice the overdose death rate of matched veterans who stayed homeless, over two years (American Journal of Public Health, 2026).
  • Seattle has seen it: in July 2023 a woman's fentanyl death at DESC's Hobson Place went unnoticed for six days under a 48-hour check policy. DESC now checks every resident in person daily.
  • Housing doesn't make use worse or better. Reviews of Housing First find more days housed and no clear change in drug use. People who use drugs keep their housing at similar rates to those who don't.

What makes a room safe enough:

  • Methadone or buprenorphine on site: after an overdose, methadone cut the risk of dying of an opioid overdose by 59% and buprenorphine by 38%. Only 11–17% of people got them.
  • Daily in-person checks, and naloxone in every hallway.
  • Motion sensors in rooms or bathrooms (Brave): about $2,000 to install and $500 a year. For 1,500 rooms that's about $3M once and $0.75M a year estimate. The only evaluation is by people tied to the company, with no comparison group.
  • Overdose-response phone lines for people using alone. Canada's line logged 53 callers who became unresponsive; all survived.

What this changes in the plan.

  • Point the rooms at the people who won't take a mat: the chronically unsheltered and the camps being cleared. Don't convert every group bed.
  • Expect rooms to cut the number outside by a third to two-thirds, about half in the middle, not to zero estimate. Of 1,000 people offered rooms, roughly 100–300 say no and 175–360 of those who come in leave within a year, so about 300–650 are outside a year later unless offers repeat. Denver's 61% sits toward the good end of that range.
  • Budget overdose safety as part of the room, not as optional treatment: daily checks, naloxone, sensors, and same-day buprenorphine or methadone.
  • Measure what nobody publishes: acceptance by type of offer, returns to the street, housing a year later, and overdose deaths per 1,000 room-years.

The short answer

  1. Seattle needs about 3,500 rooms with a door at full scale, and about 1,500 to start. The first 1,500 cover the people camping at schools, parks and the other public places. Mayor Wilson's 960 planned beds count toward it.
  2. The first 1,500 can be paid for with money Seattle already has:
    • Buy, not build. Spend the city's JumpStart housing money on buying motels and older apartment buildings, starting with the $65M just freed up, instead of building new ones.
    • Stop closing tiny home villages. Keep every village open until rooms replace it.
    Buying the buildings costs about $80–135M once. Paying to run the new rooms, about $30–50M a year, is still an open question.
  3. Full scale is the hard part. Running 3,500 rooms plus the exits behind them costs about $175–245M a year. Seattle's entire proposed 2027 homelessness budget is $183.5M. Phase 2 needs roughly $80–130M a year of new or redirected money.
  4. Denver did not wait for a room before enforcing. It enforced its camping ban the whole time and closed some camps with no housing offered. It also had more than 3,000 congregate beds a night to fall back on. Seattle's shelters are 94% full, so Seattle's version has to hold back a reserve of rooms for the ladder, and open public places zone by zone as rooms allow.
  5. This is a rooms-first plan, not a treatment plan. Treatment money already exists in county levies voters approved, so the plan pays for rooms and the ladder and connects people to that treatment. The weak spots are the last rung (prosecution) and the first rung on days with no room to offer.
  6. Rooms clear streets; they don't cure addiction. Seattle should plan for longer stays and recovery-housing exits, and measure what Denver doesn't (details).

The Denver plan, in one paragraph

Denver did four things at once. It bought and leased hotels and built villages, about 1,284 private rooms in 16 months, because a room with a door that locks is the offer people accept. It enforced a 2012 camping ban with a ladder: a spoken warning, a written warning and outreach before a ticket, with a sergeant signing off on any citation. It resolved camps by name: everyone on the list got a room, then the block was fenced, patrolled and kept closed. And one office reported to the mayor against a public number, posted monthly on the city's All In Mile High progress dashboard. Street homelessness fell 61% from 2022 to 2026. Seattle's rose 54%.

Did Denver only cite someone when it had a room to offer?

No. "No enforcement until there's a room for everyone" has been Seattle's reason to wait.

Where the money comes from

Two moves pay for the first 1,500 rooms with money Seattle already has.

1. Buy, not build

Spend the city's JumpStart housing money on buying motels and older apartment buildings instead of building new ones.

  • What the money is: JumpStart is Seattle's payroll tax on large employers. In the 2027 proposed budget, $140.2M of it goes to the Office of Housing, which mostly lends it to nonprofits to build new affordable buildings. Buying existing buildings is already one of the office's normal uses.
  • Start now: the 2027 budget frees up $65M of last year's JumpStart construction money that was never committed to a project. At $150–250k a room, that buys about 260–430 motel rooms.
  • Why buy: a motel on Aurora costs roughly $90–180k a room and is ready in weeks. A new building costs several hundred thousand dollars a unit and takes three to five years.
  • The trade: fewer new affordable apartments later. No one loses a home they have now.
  • Who fights it: the affordable-housing builders (Housing Development Consortium, Plymouth, Community Roots).
  • If more is needed: council-issued bonds ($100M costs about $7.7M a year for 20 years), or buildings the county is selling, such as the Inn at Queen Anne (reportedly listed for sale).

2. Stop closing tiny home villages

  • What's happening: Seattle opened 8 villages since 2023 (281 units) and closed 5 (about 200 units), mostly because land leases ended, not because the money ran out.
  • Why it's cheap: at about $28k a unit a year, keeping 200 units open costs about $5–6M a year, the cheapest locking doors the city has.
  • How: the council's spring 2026 law lets the city lease land for villages directly and allows villages of up to 150 people. Use it to find the next site before a lease ends.

Still open: paying to run the new rooms

Running the ~540 bought rooms, plus the reserve and exits, costs about $30–50M a year. Without a new tax or staff cuts, these come closest:

What we looked at and set aside

  • Turning group shelters into private rooms. A room costs more than a group bed, and with shelters 94% full, the people who lose a bed go outside. Only worth doing where a shelter can move into a bought motel with no net loss of beds.
  • Moving people on from permanent housing. Real, but each person who moves out needs a federal housing voucher, and those are scarce. Perhaps 250–500 extra openings a year countywide if the housing authorities set vouchers aside.
  • New revenue for full scale. The mayor's budget names a local capital-gains tax, which needs the state's permission.

About the "$250 million unspent." It isn't money in an account. An independent review found the regional authority's budgets assumed about $1B in grants over six years but spent about $750M; the city's share of the gap was about $110M. Most of it lapsed or was never drawn, and federal money can't be recovered. The authority owes the county about $17M. Treat it as an accountability story ("spend what we're given"), not a funding source.

Addiction

  • Denver has published no count of how many residents were addicted. Neither the auditor's March 2026 report nor Urban's December 2025 evaluation reports substance use among participants. Statewide, about 24% of homeless Coloradans self-report chronic substance use on the count night, and 43% in the state's service records (weak secondary source).
  • Deaths: Denver's homelessness chief testified that 19 people died at program sites, 12 of them from overdoses. Sources disagree on whether those deaths were in 2024 or 2025. Narcan is at every site. Citywide, Denver had 515 overdose deaths in 2025, two-thirds involving fentanyl.
  • Thin treatment on site:
    • Only the Aspen hotel has full-time health providers; other sites get a weekly mobile medical visit.
    • Urban found "limited access to behavioral health services," worst in the hotels and villages.
    • Hotels saw "more frequent security incidents, overdoses."
  • Relapse risk at the exit: when the city considered cutting rapid rehousing from 12 months to 6, Councilmember Gilmore warned, "There will be relapse." Urban adds that a small share of people can't manage daily living and are "unlikely to stabilize in rapid rehousing, or in some cases, even in supportive housing."

What this means for Seattle. There's no evidence Denver's rooms get people off fentanyl, and its main exit, a year of paid rent, suits active fentanyl users worst.

  • Plan for longer stays. If rooms turn over once a year instead of twice, the same 3,500 rooms serve fewer people.
  • Budget different exits. Fund recovery housing and supportive housing openings, not only rapid rehousing; the $30–60M exits line is the least certain number in the plan.
  • Measure what Denver doesn't: a one-year housing check for everyone who leaves a room, plus deaths and returns to the street, in the monthly report.

Rooms first, not treatment

This plan pays for rooms and the ladder, and nothing else. Treatment is already funded by money voters approved:

  • the county's Crisis Care Centers levy ($1.25B through 2032);
  • the county's behavioral-health sales tax (MIDD);
  • Medicaid, which pays for case management and medical respite inside a room but not the rent;
  • opioid settlement money.

The plan's job is to put a same-day treatment offer on every rung. Today that means walk-in addiction medicine at ORCA, buprenorphine from the fire department's program, and diversion through LEAD.

In 2027 the treatment rung will be thin: Seattle's crisis center doesn't open until late 2027, and there are 16 secure detox beds for the whole county. For some people a room isn't enough, so the plan still has to budget recovery-housing exits.

Two things to borrow from San Francisco

San Francisco is closer to Seattle than Denver is: mild winters, people outside all year, and, until 2024, little enforcement. Its unsheltered count fell 22% from 2024 to 2026 (4,354 to 3,400) while King County's rose 21%. San Francisco also moved its count from night to early morning, so the size of that drop is uncertain.

1. A place police can drop someone off in minutes

Officer time is the ladder's scarcest resource (what Seattle is missing). Today an officer with someone in crisis or high on the street has two places to take them, jail or an emergency room, and either can cost hours.

WhatSan Francisco's 822 Geary stabilization unit
What it isOpened April 2025 in the Tenderloin: 16 beds, open 24/7, stays of up to 23 hours. A nurse and mental-health staff are always there and a doctor is on call. Police, paramedics and street crisis teams can drop people off; people can also walk in.
Officer timeDrop-offs fell from about six hours to about two minutes (city figure).
ResultsAbout 50% of people admitted connect to ongoing care, against about 10% at the city's sobering centers (city figure).
How it opened fastMayor Lurie signed a fentanyl emergency ordinance in February 2025 that let the city skip normal contracting. The center opened about two months later in a building the city had bought in 2021 for $6.3M and left empty, leased to the operator for $1 a month. Its services are billed to Medi-Cal.
The companionThe RESET center, a $14.5M pilot opened May 2026. Police arrest people for public intoxication and take them there instead of jail. They can leave, but refusing the center means jail, and a new arrest brings them back. Nearly 400 referrals to other services in its first months; no outcome data yet.

King County already has one of these: the Connections center in Kirkland, open since August 2024, with a police entrance and drop-offs of about seven minutes, but police bring only about 13% of its admissions. Seattle's levy-funded center at 1145 Broadway opens at the end of 2027 at the earliest.

What Seattle should take from it. Don't wait for late 2027. Ask the county to open an interim police drop-off unit in Seattle in 2027, at San Francisco's scale (about 16 beds), in a building the city or county already owns, paid from the Crisis Care Centers levy and Medicaid. Put "minutes per police drop-off" in the monthly report. A RESET-style center would give the last rung somewhere to go besides diversion or jail, but it depends on police making the arrest; watch San Francisco's results before copying it.

2. The RV path: Seattle just passed San Francisco's version

San Francisco passed its law in July 2025 and began enforcing it on November 1, 2025. It's a ladder for vehicles:

  1. Count first. Outreach teams found about 500 lived-in RVs and gave permits to the people already living in them (270 to 322, depending on the source).
  2. Two-hour limit for large vehicles citywide. Permit holders are exempt for six months if they work with housing services.
  3. Tickets, then towing for everyone else: $108 fines, then impound.
  4. A way out: the city buys back vehicles (49 so far), offers housing vouchers, and opened a shelter that takes RV residents (Jerrold Commons).

Results: lived-in large vehicles are down more than 42% since the law passed, by the city's count, to about 315 in May 2026. The city says 132 families have moved into housing or shelter since October. Weak spots: housing came slowly (82 of 271 permit holders matched after five months), and many people couldn't get a permit because their vehicle didn't run or its registration had lapsed.

On October 20, 2026, the Seattle council passed Councilmember Strauss's package, 6–3 on the parking rules, with the same structure: a one-time census, permits for people already living in RVs, a two-hour limit for everyone else, loss of the permit after six refused shelter offers, eight more outreach workers, and about $5M pledged for a safe lot and the permits. Mayor Wilson has "very serious concerns" about cost and legal risk, and the implementation plan isn't due until July 2027.

What Seattle should take from it. Back the package, and fix the gaps San Francisco found: add a buyback offer, make permits reachable for vehicles that don't run or aren't registered, have the Glassyard safe lot and a shelter that takes RV residents open before towing starts, and publish the occupied-vehicle count every quarter the way San Francisco does.

Enforcing before there are enough rooms

Even with Phase 1 on schedule, Seattle will have about 1,500 rooms for 5,600 or more people outside through 2028, with shelters already full. Waiting for enough rooms means waiting most of a decade.

  1. Open places zone by zone, as fast as there are rooms. Start with schools and child care: about 110 places and perhaps 250 people estimate. Wilson's 960 beds alone can cover that in months. Then playgrounds and parks, then transit and libraries. Each month the report says which zones opened and how many rooms were set aside for them.
  2. Keep a reserve of empty rooms. Hold 10–15% of new rooms empty for ladder offers in the zones being opened. The cost is in the Phase 1 budget. This replaces the slack Denver got from its congregate beds.
  3. When there's no room, make the best offer available, and say so. That means a village spot, a congregate bed, or a safe lot for vehicles. The council's spring 2026 law already allows villages of up to 150 people and one site of up to 250 per district.
  4. Be honest about where people go. In the transition, some people will camp in less important places, like industrial edges with little foot traffic, instead of next to a school or on the water at Green Lake. The ordinance names where camping isn't allowed. It doesn't approve everywhere else; outreach, not removal, is the response there.
  5. Decide now what happens on the fifth contact. In order:
    • a stay-out order from that property, issued by the property owner (the school district or Parks), which doesn't need a prosecutor to issue;
    • a citation that is dismissed if the person accepts a room or treatment;
    • booking, since the county has no misdemeanor booking limits now.
    The weak link is filing charges, which today depends on a city attorney who diverts these cases. Write the ordinance so most of the force comes from the stay-out order and the property owner, as Denver's trespass enforcement did.
  6. Build in safety valves.
    • No closures at or below freezing without an indoor offer; that was Johnston's own rule.
    • Store belongings; don't throw them away.
    • Count where people end up, so the monthly report shows displacement as well as clearances.
  7. Check the law before the pledge goes out.
    • HB 2489 (2026): a state bill, backed by the ACLU, that would bar enforcing camping bans without adequate alternative shelter. It died in House Rules in 2026; expect it back in January 2027.
    • Precedents in Washington: Tacoma passed buffers around schools, parks and libraries in October 2025, apparently with no shelter-availability condition. The state Supreme Court struck down Spokane's 1,000-foot ban in April 2025, but only because it was passed by citizen initiative; the court left open whether enforcing without shelter is allowed. So the ordinance has to come from the council, not the ballot.
    • Seattle's appeal of the obstruction-rule ruling (Kitcheon).
    • The Coalition on Homelessness's suit against Burien's 500-foot buffers around schools, parks and libraries, which is close to what the pledge describes.

The political reality (Mike's read)

  1. What a politician fears most is money spent against them. Next is losing money that already backs them. So the pledge needs a credible threat of spending against non-signers.
    • The 2025 caveat: the pro-Harrell committee outspent the pro-Wilson one $1.8M to $86K and lost, and Amazon's $1.45M in 2019 backfired.
    • Money works as teeth only alongside a list of verified voters in each district, and messengers who aren't business.
  2. The other side's power is structural, not accidental. District elections, a redistricting that protects the progressive wing, and organizing where nobody organized before, like ballot-printing tables on street corners.
    • Organizing by place answers it: a captain at each school or park, and the families who walk their kids past a camp, such as immigrant parents near Jose Rizal Park on Beacon Hill (District 2).
  3. Expect the framing attack. It will sound like "the tech guys took the right-wing pill." The answers:
    • Denver's mayor is a Democrat and a former school principal.
    • This plan turns sweep money into rooms, which is what Services Not Sweeps asked for.
    • Make it about kids walking to school, not a fight with the left.
  4. Building a list creates an audience, not an organizing moment. The residents' concrete blocks on Aurora were a moment.
    • Moments coming up: the final budget vote around November 20, 2026; the D5 special election November 3; the first day of school; each monthly report.

What changes in the pledge

Suggested edits to the pledge, numbered as in an earlier five-item draft:

Why public places first

Seattle's trap has been treating rooms as a condition for enforcing at all. Rooms never arrive fast enough, so the only tool left is the same-day sidewalk sweep, and people move a few hundred meters and come back. Public places are a much smaller job than the whole city, and the part neighbors care about most. The public spaces page counts how many encampments and drug zones are in or near them, by district.

The tradeoff. Some people will move a few blocks rather than inside. The Denver analysis found the median person displaced by a Seattle sweep was about 700 meters away a year later.

The enforcement ladder

Written into the ordinance, so every step can be counted. Since the Supreme Court's Grants Pass decision in 2024, federal law doesn't require a shelter bed before enforcing, so the first step offers the best shelter that exists that day, a room whenever there is one.

Denver rarely used the threat. In 2023, its police logged 297 spoken warnings, 3 written warnings and 14 camping tickets.

What council can and can't do

  • Council can: pass the ordinance naming public places and writing in the ladder, fund the outreach-and-officer teams, move money from sweeps to rooms, change siting rules (see possible village sites), and require the monthly report.
  • Council can't make the city attorney prosecute. The city attorney is elected separately and doesn't prosecute camping today. The pledge needs a city attorney version too; the next race for that office is in 2029.
  • Seattle doesn't need a new drug law. Washington made public drug use a crime again in 2023, after the Blake ruling, and Seattle adopted it into city code that fall. The gap is enforcement.
  • State courts are still a risk. Grants Pass settled the federal question, but Washington courts can rule under the state constitution, as a 2023 ruling against Seattle's sidewalk-obstruction rule did.

The 2023 drug law, Seattle's ordinance adopting it and the 2029 city attorney race are from memory. The ladder's deadlines (72 hours, 48 hours) are suggestions, not Denver's numbers.
