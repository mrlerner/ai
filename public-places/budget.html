What Does Seattle Spend on Homelessness?

Updated October 31, 2026. Figures are the 2026 adopted budget unless marked otherwise.

The short answer: the city's homelessness budget is $229 million, but that's only about a third of the money. Counting federal, state, county and private dollars too, about $615 million a year is spent on homelessness in Seattle (our estimate). The biggest share pays for permanent supportive housing, not shelter.

Counting every source, about $615 million a year is spent on homelessness in Seattle.

No government publishes this number, so we built it from budgets, federal award lists, housing-authority plans and nonprofit tax filings. It's an estimate with a range of about $550–690M. The city's own budget is the biggest single piece, but it's only about a third of the total.

Layer 1$228.7M

City of Seattle homelessness budget

Shelter and service contracts, encampment response and cleanup. This is the number the City Council controls and the one usually quoted.

Layer 2~$265M

Other government money spent in Seattle

Housing-authority vouchers, the city's affordable-housing office, the county's housing sales tax, federal grants, state grants and Medicaid. Most of it pays for supportive housing, and none of it is counted as "homelessness spending."

Layer 3~$120M

Private giving

Donations to providers such as Union Gospel Mission, Mary's Place and DESC, after taking out the government money in their budgets.

How we count, so no dollar is counted twice

  1. Seattle only. Where money is spent across King County, we count only Seattle's part: by building where we know it, otherwise 50–70%, Seattle's usual share of the county's homeless population.
  2. Count each dollar once, where it's finally spent, and credit it to whoever raised it. State money that passes through the county and then the regional agency is counted once, as state money.
  3. Yearly running costs only. Money to buy or build buildings (about $65M a year, almost all for supportive housing) is shown separately.
  4. For nonprofits, count only private donations. About 60–70% of the big providers' budgets is government contracts, Medicaid billing and rent paid with vouchers, which is already counted above. Grants from funders like Ballmer or United Way show up as donations to providers, so they aren't added again.

The city raises less than half the money. Federal programs and private donors together add almost as much.

Everything except the city budget is our estimate. Housing-authority vouchers: SHA's 2026 budget lists $8.4M for veterans' vouchers, $8.0M for family and foster-youth vouchers, $4.9M for disability vouchers and ~$10M for emergency vouchers; about 2,526 project-based vouchers in supportive and homeless buildings are priced at SHA's average voucher cost. Office of Housing: $27.0M in 2025 for supportive-housing staff pay across 3,963 apartments, plus operating subsidies the city doesn't total. Health Through Housing: $39.4M of 2024 operating costs countywide, 57% of operating units in Seattle. Medicaid and state grants: statewide figures scaled to Seattle. "What it buys" uses the program mix of Seattle's own regional-agency contracts and splits private money roughly $55M shelter, $30M supportive housing and $35M services. Not included: clinical mental-health and addiction treatment, police, fire and EMS time, jail and hospital costs, and loans or investments (Microsoft's $750M housing fund is about 97% loans).

Most of the city's $229 million goes through one department, and most of that is passed on to the regional agency.

The $228.7M citywide total and the $172.4M HSD figure come from the 2026 Adopted Budget. The Unified Care Team amounts come from Council provisos on $29.9M across seven departments. The ~$32M remainder is the difference between the citywide total and what can be itemized.

The Human Services Department's homelessness budget rose 24% from 2025 ($138.9M → $172.4M). It includes $11.5M for prevention and rental assistance, $20M set aside in case federal funding is cut, and $7.8M to open 155 new shelter units.

County, state and federal money shows up mainly in the regional agency, and much of the county's share is really state money.

The city's own $229M is almost entirely local: the General Fund, a short-term rental tax and the Housing Levy. Of the $126.8M it sends to KCRHA, only $3.9M (about 3%) is federal grant money.

What KCRHA buys: enhanced shelter (45%), permanent supportive housing (12%), rapid rehousing (12%), tiny-home villages (9%), its own admin and operations (7%), transitional housing (4%), and smaller programs such as day centers, diversion and safe parking. Most of the money outside the city budget pays for supportive housing, and none of it is called homelessness spending.

~$65M a year

Seattle Housing Authority vouchers

Federal rent subsidies set aside for homeless people: about 2,500 vouchers attached to supportive-housing buildings, plus vouchers for veterans, families and emergency cases. The housing authority isn't part of the city budget.

~$45M a year

Seattle Office of Housing

Pay for supportive-housing staff in 58 buildings (3,963 apartments, $27M in 2025) and operating subsidies, from the Housing Levy and JumpStart. The office also spends $34–49M a year building new supportive housing. Its $349.5M budget is mostly affordable housing in general.

~$30M a year

Federal HUD grants outside the regional agency

The region gets $67M a year from HUD's Continuum of Care program. KCRHA holds $27M; King County holds $39M, mostly for supportive housing and rent help. About $30M of the county's share is spent in Seattle. HUD tried to cut this in 2025; King County sued and won in June 2026.

~$26M a year

King County Health Through Housing

A 0.1% countywide sales tax that buys and runs supportive housing. Nine of its buildings, 772 apartments, are in Seattle. Countywide it spent $39M running buildings in 2024, plus $24M on bond payments. Its often-quoted $180M is a two-year budget.

~$33M a year

Medicaid and state grants

Medicaid pays providers per visit for housing support services (~$22M in Seattle), and the state pays supportive-housing buildings an operating grant (~$11M). Both go to the same nonprofits that run city-funded buildings, but they pay for different costs, so they add up rather than overlap.

~$17M a year

Veterans and county levies

VA grants for veterans' rent help and per-diem beds (~$9M), and county levies for veterans' and behavioral-health housing (~$8M).

Private donors give about $120 million a year, but most nonprofit spending is really government money.

How to say it without double counting

  1. The city budgets $229M for homelessness. That's what the City Council controls.
  2. Counting every source, about $615M a year is spent in Seattle: about $265M more from other government programs and about $120M from private donors. Call it an estimate.
  3. Most of that money pays for permanent supportive housing (~$231M), not shelter (~$143M), plus about $65M a year to build more supportive housing. The city's own budget is the reverse: mostly shelter.

Don't add KCRHA's $202.9M to the city's $228.7M; that counts Seattle's $117.6M twice. And don't add a nonprofit's whole budget: most of it is government money already counted.
