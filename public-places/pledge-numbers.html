The short version

  1. Seattle doesn't publish the two numbers the pledge asks for. The count of people outside comes every two years, countywide, and no Seattle figure has been published for 2026. The mayor's page promising 1,000 new units has no tracker of units opened. We had to add up the 2026 openings ourselves from news reports.
  2. Some pieces exist. The city's Unified Care Team reports tents, RVs, shelter offers and removals to the Council each quarter. It counts tents, not people, and says nothing about rooms opened or what happens to people after.
  3. Other cities show it can be done. Denver updates a public dashboard every month. San Francisco counts tents and vehicles every quarter and posts shelter occupancy every weekday. Houston reports whether people are still housed two years later.
  4. No city reports everything. Deaths, returns to the street and cost per room are missing almost everywhere. Denver's own auditor found its dashboard counted temporary stays as housing.
  5. We propose twelve measures, each one already published by at least one city or by Seattle itself, in one report, every quarter, for the city limits.

Why publishing matters

  • A promise without a tracker can't be checked. The mayor promised 1,000 new units in her first year. Ten months in, the city's own page still shows the goal and no count. The roughly 280 opened is our tally from news reports and announcements.
  • Denver ran its plan against one public number. One office reported to the mayor against a target, posted monthly. Street homelessness fell 61% from 2022 to 2026.
  • Numbers catch what isn't working. San Diego's auditor found that after its camping ban, people sleeping outside citywide didn't go down, encampment reports rose 13%, and reports near schools and parks with signs “did not appear to substantially change.” Without the audit, downtown's cleared sidewalks would have been the whole story.
  • It's what voters can hold a council member to. Items 1 and 2 of the pledge only mean something if anyone can see, each quarter, whether public places are clearing and rooms are opening.

The quarterly report does hold numbers worth keeping. In 2025 outreach made 7,090 shelter offers; 1,846 were accepted, 1,011 people confirmed enrolled, and 31 went on to permanent housing. In the second quarter of 2026 outreach had an average of 8.2 beds a weekday it could actually offer. Shelter was offered at only 41% of removals for hazards and obstructions in 2025.

How other cities report

Denver: one dashboard, every month

The mayor's office posts a one-page dashboard on the first Wednesday of every month, from the region's homeless services database.

  • People moved into shelter with their own door: 9,272 people since July 18, 2023, by year.
  • People moved into housing: 8,605 since July 2023, by year.
  • How people leave shelter: 53% positive, 32% negative (jail, back to homelessness, or death), 15% unknown, with the definitions on a second page.
  • The count of people outside: 1,423 in January 2023 to 518 in January 2026, with a line marking when the program began.

What Denver's auditor found. In March 2026 the city auditor found that “moved to housing” included temporary stays, such as transitional programs, self-paid motels and short stays with family, which federal rules count as temporary. Someone who becomes homeless again stays in the housed count, and one-year follow-up “is not currently tracked.” Costs were under-reported by about $20.1M. The auditor recommended separating permanent from temporary housing on the dashboard. The mayor's office disagreed.

The lesson: publish monthly like Denver, but separate permanent from temporary housing, track returns, and let an auditor check the definitions.

San Francisco: a quarterly street count and daily occupancy

  • A count of tents, structures and vehicles every three months, mapped by neighborhood, with history back to 2020. September 2026: 148 tents and structures and 249 vehicles, 193 of them RVs, campers or vans. May 2026 was the lowest since counting began in 2018.
  • Shelter capacity and occupancy, refreshed every weekday, by type of shelter: 3,039 of 3,416 places full (90%) on November 4, 2026. The city's target is 90–95%.
  • What's missing: people (it counts tents), beds opened against beds closed, exits, returns and deaths. The difference matters: San Francisco opened 863 shelter beds in 16 months but closed others, for a net gain of 403.

San Diego: a monthly downtown count, and an auditor

The Downtown San Diego Partnership, a nonprofit business district, has counted people sleeping outside downtown every month since 2013, by neighborhood. It converts tents and vehicles into people at 1.75 a tent and 2.03 a lived-in vehicle. The city auditor's April 2026 audit of the camping ban measured what the city didn't: citywide counts, encampment reports near schools and parks, days to respond to a report, and how often police contacts ended in a citation.

Houston: what happens two years later

Houston's Coalition for the Homeless, which runs the region's homeless system, posts monthly dashboards and an annual report. It reports how many people were housed (2,135 households in 2025), how many became homeless for the first time, and whether people stayed housed: “85% … remained stably housed over a two-year period.” Its homepage gives a different figure, about 95%, so even the best reporter needs one consistent definition. Houston reports for its whole region, not the city limits, and has no quarterly street count.

Los Angeles and Phoenix

Los Angeles shows the risk of running totals. Its Inside Safe page counts people moved indoors and housed, but not how many went back outside. A news analysis in April 2026 found about 40% had.

Phoenix publishes its count for the city limits, not just the county: people outside fell from 3,541 to 3,093 in 2026, and more than half of its homeless residents are now sheltered.

How to count people outside every quarter

The county's full count happens every two years and takes months to publish. A quarterly number for Seattle needs a lighter method that's the same every time.

  1. Count tents, structures and lived-in vehicles each quarter, street by street, the way San Francisco does. Seattle's outreach teams already count tents and RVs for the Council.
  2. Convert them to people with published factors, the way the Downtown San Diego Partnership does (1.75 people a tent, 2.03 a vehicle), and check the factors against each two-year county count.
  3. Keep a list of people by name. Denver works from a daily by-name list. The Built for Zero standard asks that the list be updated at least monthly and that each month's change equal the people who came in minus the people who left; if it doesn't, the list isn't complete.
  4. Flag every change of method on the report itself. San Francisco moved its count from night to early morning in 2026, which makes its drop hard to read.

Rules for honest numbers

  • City limits, every time. Seattle's numbers are buried in countywide ones. Phoenix publishes its own.
  • Net, not gross. Rooms opened minus rooms closed. Seattle opened 8 villages since 2023 and closed 5.
  • Permanent separate from temporary, using the federal definitions.
  • People, not running totals. A running total of people moved indoors hides the people who went back out.
  • A date and a definition on every number, and the full history kept public. San Francisco's occupancy page says historical data “is not available.”
  • One office responsible, and an auditor who checks it. Denver's auditor caught what its dashboard didn't say.
  • Places, never people. Maps show where encampments are, not who lives there.
