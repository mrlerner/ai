How Much Should a Shelter Bed Come With?

Draft, November 1, 2026. Builds on What Else Could Seattle Buy?

Every new place Seattle opens comes with the same full package: staff on site around the clock, a case manager for every 20 or so residents, a behavioral health specialist, meals, plumbed showers and grid power. The yearly money that runs 1,000 places at today's standard would run about 1,770 at a medium standard or 3,540 at a low one (estimate).

$38M a year is what 1,000 places cost at today's village standard, close to the $37M the 2027 budget sets aside for about 1,000 new beds. "All in" spreads the cost to build a village over 5 years and to buy a motel over 30, at 4.5% interest. Hotels already have plumbing and power, so only the staffing and meals change.

Mixing levels by need costs about the same as all medium

About 30% of people outside need a lot of support, 25% some, and 45% mostly need a place (our rough split from the 2026 county count). Matching each group to high, medium and low averages about $22k a place in villages, or ~1,760 places for $38M, +760, with full services for the people who need them.

Today's standard is the full package, at every site, for everyone.

The city's first 500 new places were aimed at people who were chronically homeless with high needs. The budget director put the average at about $28k a place a year, with some sites above $45k.

Pallet, which made many of the tiny homes, required 24/7 staff, meals, hygiene, transportation and case management before it would ship, and LIHI makes case management mandatory in its villages. Both said shelter without services doesn't move people into housing and neighbors won't accept a village without staff. The result is no lighter tier for people who don't need all of it. Pallet shut down in September 2026, so its rule no longer binds anyone.

People, not tiny homes, are two-thirds of the running cost.

At today's standard, staff on site and case management are about $19k of the $28k it takes to run a village place each year. Cutting staffing is what moves the total; switching from plumbing to portable toilets saves far less.

Staff costs assume about $65k a year per full-time job with benefits ($55k for a site host), and 4.2 full-time jobs to cover one post around the clock. Case managers are $85k. The high totals match the city's $28k average and DESC's $39–45k hotel figures; the low village total matches San Diego's staffed Safe Sleeping sites, about $8–11.5k a tent. Medium is built up from the parts and has no direct benchmark.

Generators and portable toilets are faster, not cheaper. Use them to open, then hook up.

A grid connection or sewer hookup can take months of permits and utility work, and it's a big part of why a village takes 5–12 months to open.

Open fast, then hook up

Open on portable toilets and a rented generator, then switch to grid power and a hygiene trailer within a few months. A generator heating every tiny home for a year costs about $80k more than the grid for 50 tiny homes. This is separate from the service level: a high-service village can open on a generator, and a low-service one can run on the grid.

The extra places pay off unless lighter services cut exits by almost half.

Cheaper places help only if people still move on to housing. Evidence on how much exits fall with lighter services is thin, so these are the break-even points.

Medium villages: 77% more places

Medium moves more people into housing than high unless exits per place fall by more than 43%. Visiting case managers at 1 per 40 still meet every resident every week or two.

Low villages: 3.5 times the places

Low comes out ahead unless exits fall by more than 72%. That's possible: basic group shelter sends only 3–6% of the people who leave to housing, against 34–46% for villages.

Hotels: 45% to 95% more rooms

Medium hotel rooms break even at a 32% drop in exits, and low at 49%. The margin is smaller because a hotel's building costs don't fall with the service level.

Even when exits fall, everyone in the extra places is inside tonight. A lighter tier also frees today's full-service places for the people who need them most. Bayside, a high-service village, took about 2½ months to fill because intake ran 7–10 people a week.

What no standard should cut

  • A door that locks, and fire-safe spacing between tiny homes. People rarely turn down a tiny house; they often turn down a mat.
  • Someone accountable on site around the clock. It's what neighbors are promised and what makes siting possible.
  • Fencing and a monitored gate. Cheap, and it's what separates a village from an encampment.
  • Sanitation every day. Portable toilets serviced often enough to stay clean, and hand-washing.
  • Naloxone and overdose checks. Fentanyl makes using alone dangerous, which is why even the low hotel standard keeps a 24/7 desk.
  • A path out. Even at low, a navigator visits weekly, and residents are expected to take housing when it's offered.

What these numbers leave out

  • Every line is our estimate. Component costs are built up, not taken from contracts.
  • Land. A village needs a site and a lease at every service level.
  • Vehicles need lots, not tiny homes. About 23% of people outside live in cars or RVs; safe lots cost less than any of these.
  • Treatment isn't included at any level. Detox and psychiatric beds are a separate cost.
  • Neighborhood acceptance may fall with lighter staffing.
