Protect Public Places Pledge

Keep Seattle's public places — schools, parks, transit and the places we share — free of camping and drug use, starting now. Offer everyone a room with a door they can lock. Report every month on whether it's working.

As a member of the Seattle City Council, I will vote to:

  1. Name the public places that must stay clear, in law. Schools and child care, parks, playgrounds and libraries, transit, and the places all of Seattle shares.
  2. Enforce no-camping and no-public-drug-use rules there with a ladder, starting in year one. Schools and child care first, then the other public places. Every step comes with an offer of a room or treatment, so help always comes before a penalty.
  3. Keep cleared places clear. Once a place is cleared, it stays clear, with a quick response when a tent or drug use comes back.
  4. Build rooms people will accept, alongside enforcement, without waiting for them. Make a private room with a door that locks the standard offer, open rooms fast enough to keep up, and make every room a step toward housing.
  5. Publish a monthly progress report on encampments. One public count of encampments in Seattle each month, by district and by type of public place, with clear goals for bringing it down and how many offers of rooms and treatment were accepted.

Why public places first

Seattle's trap has been treating rooms as a condition for enforcing at all. Rooms never arrive fast enough, so the only tool left is the same-day sidewalk sweep, and people move a few hundred meters and come back. Starting with public places breaks that link: it's a much smaller job than the whole city, and it's the part neighbors care about most.

The tradeoff, said out loud. Some people will move a few blocks rather than inside. The Denver analysis found the median person displaced by a Seattle sweep was about 700 meters away a year later. The pledge's position is that a school or a park matters more than a side street, and that's why item 4, rooms people will accept, isn't optional.

The enforcement ladder

Written into the ordinance, so it's the same everywhere and every step can be counted. Since the Supreme Court's Grants Pass decision in 2024, federal law doesn't require a shelter bed before enforcing, so the first step offers the best shelter that exists that day, a room whenever there is one.

Dealing gets no ladder. Sales at a public place mean arrest and prosecution. The ladder is for people using, not for people selling to them.

The threat is rarely used because it rarely has to be. In 2023, Denver police logged 297 spoken warnings, 3 written warnings and 14 camping tickets, while Denver cut street homelessness 61% from 2022 to 2026. Seattle's rose 54% over the same period.

What council can and can't do

  • Council can: pass the ordinance naming public places and writing in the ladder, fund the outreach-and-officer teams, move money from sweeps to rooms, change siting rules (see possible village sites), and require the monthly report.
  • Council can't make the city attorney prosecute. The city attorney is elected separately and doesn't prosecute camping today. The pledge needs a city attorney version too; the next race for that office is in 2029.
  • Seattle doesn't need a new drug law. Washington made public drug use a crime again in 2023, after the Blake ruling, and Seattle adopted it into city code that fall. The gap is enforcement.
  • State courts are still a risk. Grants Pass settled the federal question, but Washington courts can rule under the state constitution, as a 2023 ruling against Seattle's sidewalk-obstruction rule did. The ordinance needs a careful lawyer.
  • Money. The yearly cost of running rooms at Seattle's scale (about $180–200M) roughly matches what Seattle already spends on sweeps, congregate shelter and rooms (see what it costs in the Denver plan). The one-time cost of buying or leasing buildings (about $400–600M) isn't covered. The likely answers are a bond measure, or King County running some of its roughly 1,400 hotel rooms as shelter.
