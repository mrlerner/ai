Spending Options: What Else Could Seattle Buy?

Draft, October 31, 2026. Built on What Does Seattle Spend on Homelessness? Figures marked estimate are our own arithmetic.

The short answer: counting every source, about $615 million a year is spent on homelessness in Seattle, and about $450 million of it pays for places to sleep. The biggest share goes to permanent supportive apartments (~$231M), then shelter (~$143M). The city's own $229 million is the opposite: mostly shelter. Bringing the ~6,700 people still outside indoors would cost roughly another $190–390 million a year, depending on the kind of place.

Three ways to bring everyone inside

Each option gives a place to the roughly 6,700 people outside and keeps everything funded today. The difference is what kind of place they get. Percentages compare the added cost with today's ~$615M from all sources.

+$190M a year (+31%)

1. Cheapest: a bed for everyone

  • What: group shelter beds, plus safe lots for the people living in vehicles.
  • Cost: about $28k a place. Little upfront, because the buildings can be leased.
  • Speed: months.
  • The catch: many people won't come. Only 25% of shelter offers were accepted in 2024, and the most common reason for saying no was wanting a tiny house instead. Basic mats send only 3–6% of the people who leave to housing.
+$300M a year (+49%)

2. Middle: a room with a door

  • What: half tiny-home villages, half bought motels. This is Denver's approach.
  • Cost: about $45k a place, plus about $0.8B once to buy motels and build villages.
  • Speed: villages in weeks once a site is found; motels in months.
  • Why: people rarely turn down a tiny house, and villages run 90–95% full. Villages send 34–46% of the people who leave to housing, and motels 38%. That turnover lets each room serve more people over time.
+$390M a year (+63%)

3. Most expensive: an apartment for everyone

  • What: new permanent supportive apartments, the Housing First approach.
  • Cost: about $58k a place all in, including $3.4B once to build. It's the cheapest to run (about $180M a year once built), but the most expensive to build.
  • Speed: years. The county took three years to get about 1,500 units, even buying existing hotels.
  • Why: about 80% of people are still housed a year or two later. But it ends with everyone in permanent units, so new people becoming homeless need new units too.

Why not just rearrange today's money?

Because most of it is already in use. Of the ~$450M spent on places to sleep, ~$231M pays rent and staff for people living in supportive apartments now, and ~$143M runs shelter. Moving that money means putting someone else outside. And the temporary money already buys the cheap kind: shelter is about 82% of the ~$175M spent on temporary places.

What can move is the money for new buildings. About $65M a year goes to building supportive housing, roughly 130 new apartments at $500k each. Spent on motels or villages instead, it would buy about 260–430 motel rooms or 1,200–1,900 village units a year (estimate), though each still needs $25–50k a year to run.

The Discovery Institute proposes going further, by redirecting 20% of homelessness and housing money to emergency treatment shelters. Applied to ~$615M, that's about $120M a year: roughly 2,700 rooms with doors at $45k or 4,300 group beds at $28k (estimate).

What these numbers leave out

  • People keep becoming homeless. 6,700 is one night. About 54,000 people are homeless in King County at some point in a year. Options 1 and 2 only keep up if rooms turn over, which takes case managers and housing to move people into.
  • Vehicles need lots, not rooms. About 27% of people outside live in cars or RVs. Safe lots cost less than rooms, so every option would cost somewhat less than shown.
  • Budgets aren't spending. Every figure here is a budget. $110M of the $565M Seattle has awarded through the regional agency since 2021 went unused, so real spending is probably lower.
  • Treatment isn't included. About 75% of people outside have a substance use problem. Detox and psychiatric beds are a separate cost, on top of every option.
  • A real plan mixes them. The Denver plan uses rooms with doors for people who won't take a mat, keeps group beds for people who will, and uses safe lots for vehicles.
