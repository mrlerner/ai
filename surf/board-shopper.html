Board Shopper — Mid-Length Step-Down

Four candidates to slot in under the Lib Tech Pickup Stick 8'0 — built for softer, less-steep San Diego waves rather than steep North Pacific faces.

Specs & prices checked Aug 6, 2026 · ranks tailored to Matt's surfing · final call: Firewire Seaside & Beyond 7'4

The brief: step down from 8'0 to ~7'4–7'6 without paying a paddle tax. Priorities that drove the ranking: glide and glass over swell power · paddling effort is a first-class cost · 2–4 ft is the happy zone · turtle-roll volumes (duck diving is a future goal — every board this size is a turtle-roll board for everyone) · the main progression goal: easier turning, but forgiving turning — Matt wants to progress on turns on a board where it's hard to accidentally bury a rail: fuller, softer rails that engage gradually beat thin performance rails that give sharper feedback but dig when weight or timing is off. Feedback-y rails are a later-stage want · travels with his boards and doesn't want to deal with dings on the road — toughness is a stated priority.

The Stack Rank

1

Firewire Seaside & Beyond 7'4

The final pick — designed for the waves you actually surf

49.5 L 7'4 × 21¾ × 2¾ Quad $1,255 new · older-run 7'6 ~$1,025

Size run: 6'8 (40.9 L) · 7'0 (45.3 L) · 7'4 (49.5 L) — current Volcanic production; older LFT/Helium runs included other lengths (e.g. the used-market 7'6).

  • Flattest entry rocker of the four — the direct fix for what the steep-rockered Pickup Stick lacks in soft San Diego walls.
  • Designed by Rob Machado for 1–5 ft lined-up Cardiff-style surf: your 2–4 ft happy zone is its sweet spot.
  • Quad fish DNA means down-the-line speed without pumping — glide and glass, not swell power.
  • 49.5 L keeps the wave count high, and a 7'4 is easier to turtle roll than your 8'0.
  • Half a foot shorter and much less swing weight than the 8'0 — noticeably easier to turn, though the fish outline favors drawn-out flowing arcs over tighter rail-to-rail work.
  • Rail forgiveness (the turn-progression priority): full, fishy rails engage gradually and are hard to accidentally bury — turns initiate with body lean rather than a knife-edge catching. The trade is muted feedback, which is the right trade at this stage.
  • Finish note: new production is Volcanic (black basalt) only — and the black is the actual basalt fiber, not paint. Older-run Helium / white LFT builds exist in shops and San Diego's used market.
  • Construction — Helium core + Volcanic lamination: stringerless EPS with paulownia wood rails doing the flex work, wrapped in basalt cloth — actual volcanic-rock fiber, stronger than fiberglass and more elastic than carbon (and the source of the black look). Very light and springy; still hand-laminated EPS, so it needs more ding care than Thunderbolt.
  • Why it won: designed at Cardiff Reef for exactly the waves Matt surfs most; full rails and keel-quad hold give the forgiving turn profile that matters right now; Volcanic basalt keeps it on the tougher side (the Moe's durability edge is mostly airline handling — solvable with a padded bag); and a firsthand report from a friend on the 7'2 Moe — more rocker than soft San Diego waves want — broke the tiebreak the other way.

Harley Ingleby Moe 7'4

Runner-up — the wave-count and travel-toughness machine

52.9 L 7'4 × 22½ × 2⅞ 5 boxes — thruster or quad ~$1,210

Size run: 7'2 (49.4 L) · 7'4 (52.9 L) · 8'0 (61.2 L) — plus the separate Moe Mini model at 6'6 (41 L) and 6'10 (45 L).

  • Most volume of the four (52.9 L) — the smallest paddle penalty coming off the Pickup Stick, and reviewers call it the small-wave mid-length.
  • Rocker: flat through the middle (the paddle-and-glide engine) with a flicked-up nose that barely drags in trim. More curve than the Machados, but it's the small-wave-friendly kind — reviewers report early entry in knee-high mush — and the curve is what makes turning easier, which is the goal. Nothing like the Pickup Stick's continuous steep-wave rocker.
  • Thunderbolt construction is markedly more ding-resistant than Firewire's EPS builds — the best board here to fly with.
  • Flat bottom with double concave between your feet — exactly the bottom shape that makes carving accessible on a wide board. Harley's series exists to bring performance turning to mid-lengths.
  • Five FCS II boxes — thruster or quad (Harley recommends his XL quad set on the 7'4), so it's not locked into a funboard feel.
  • Rail forgiveness: rounded rails and a squash-style tail are also hard to bury — on par with the Seaside & Beyond for mistake-tolerance, and the most forgiving overall thanks to the extra volume. The rail-forgiveness priority doesn't separate the top two; wave-match does.
  • Construction — Thunderbolt Red: EPS core with internal carbon flex beams instead of a stringer; the skin is vacuum-bagged in epoxy, curing with maximum cloth and minimum brittle resin. In the water that means light but damped, with tuned spring through turns; in the board bag it's the most ding-proof build here — the no-hassle-traveling pick.
  • Why #2: still the toughest build and the biggest paddle engine here — but a friend riding the 7'2 Moe reports more rocker than soft San Diego waves want, and the Seaside & Beyond was designed at Cardiff for exactly those waves. Forgiveness now outweighs feedback in the turn-learning brief, and the Machado's full rails deliver it. A back-to-back demo can still flip this — the gap is that thin.

Firewire Taylor Jensen Pro Mid 7'4

The all-rounder — best if one board must also do the PNW

48.0 L 7'4 × 21⅞ × 2⅝ 5 boxes — single / 2+1 / quad ~$1,255

Size run: 7'2 (46.5 L) · 7'4 (48.0 L) · 7'8 (51.2 L) · 8'0 (54.5 L).

  • A longboarder's mid-length — trim-and-flow style that matches how you actually surf.
  • Five fin boxes: single fin for glassy point days, 2+1 or quad when it has push — the most versatile quiver-of-one here.
  • Rocker: the most all-round curve of the four — balanced nose-to-tail so it handles steeper, better faces. That's also exactly why it wants more push than an average soft San Diego day: more mid-board curve costs glide in mush.
  • Handles steeper, better waves than the two Machados, so it overlaps your home spots too.
  • Construction — I-Bolic core + Volcanic lamination: EPS core engineered with high-density foam structure — an 18mm I-beam center stringer, parabolic rail inserts, and a bottom "springer" — under a basalt skin and a compression-resistant deck. The stiffest, most solid-underfoot Firewire build: stable and consistent, less corky than Helium.
  • Why #4: it turns beautifully, but wants more wave energy than San Diego's average day to do it — the Xtra Cado delivers its turns in the soft stuff you'll actually be riding. Priciest of the four, and your Lib Tech covers the steep days.

Firewire Xtra Cado 7'6

The turn trainer — its performance rails just became a feature

50.6 L 7'6 × 21⅞ × 2¾ 5 boxes $995–$1,125 · 7'6 sold out direct

Size run: 6'6 (38.7 L) · 6'8 (39.3 L) · 7'0 (44.4 L) · 7'2 (47.1 L) · 7'6 (50.6 L).

  • Machado's stretched Machadocado: real glide for small, gutless waves, with more foam (50.6 L) than the Seaside & Beyond.
  • Pulled-down, thinner rails and double-concave-to-vee add turnability — the most "performance" of the mellow options.

Best turn feedback of the four — revisit when rail control is solid and sharper feedback becomes the thing that accelerates progression.

The lightest, flexiest feel of the four — and the least reinforced skin, so the most ding-prone: the weakest fit for the no-ding-hassle-while-traveling requirement.
  • Why #3 (up from #4): with easier turning now the stated goal, the pulled-down rails and vee out the tail — the easiest rail-to-rail initiation of the four — flip from liability to asset. Still behind the Moe on wave count, durability, and forgiveness while you're learning.

How the ranking was made

Weighted to how you actually surf, not spec-sheet maximalism:

  1. Wave match first. The target is soft, less-steep San Diego surf in your 2–4 ft comfort band. The two Machado shapes and the Moe are built for exactly that; the TJ Pro Mid prefers a touch more energy.
  2. Paddle effort is a first-class cost. Nothing under 48 L made the list. The Moe (52.9 L) is the paddle king; the Seaside & Beyond gives up little while shedding half a foot of length.
  3. Easier turning is the goal — and progression is reps × design. Every board here turns far easier than the 8'0 (less length, less swing weight). Beyond that, turn progression comes from turns attempted, which favors the board that catches the most waves — and from turn-friendly design: vee or double-concave bottoms and responsive flex that make getting on rail easy. The ranking weighs both.
  4. Forgiveness over performance. Glass and glide beat rail-to-rail shredding — which is why the sharper-railed Xtra Cado sits fourth despite being a superb board.
  5. It has to survive the trip. Matt travels with his boards and explicitly doesn't want ding hassle on the road — a dinged EPS board mid-trip means finding a repair shop or sitting out sessions. Thunderbolt > Firewire EPS here, the Moe's strongest card. A day bag is still worth it.

Final call (revised Aug 8, 2026): the Firewire Seaside & Beyond 7'4 in Volcanic. Four things flipped the top two: it was designed at Cardiff Reef for exactly the soft, lined-up San Diego waves this board will surf most; its full rails and keel-quad hold give the forgiving turn profile that matters at this stage (forgiveness over feedback); Volcanic basalt puts it on the tougher side of Firewire's builds — the Moe's durability edge is mostly airline handling, solvable with a padded travel bag; and a firsthand report from a friend riding the 7'2 Moe says it carries more rocker than soft San Diego days want. Order of preference: Seaside & Beyond 7'4 → Moe 7'4 → Xtra Cado → TJ Pro Mid 7'4. If a back-to-back demo on a small day flips the top two back, trust the demo.
