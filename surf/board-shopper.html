Board Shopper — Mid-Lengths

Two questions, two rankings: (1) a board like the Lib Tech Pickup Stick 8'0 but better for San Diego, to keep there; (2) the future step down, once the 8'0 checklist is done.

Specs & prices checked Aug 6 – Sep 25, 2026 · re-organized Sep 25, 2026 · Lowrider 8'0 Sep 26 · San Diego pick: Rusty Lowrider 8'0 · future step down: Harley Ingleby Moe 7'4 (on hold until the 8'0 checklist is done)

1 · Like the Lib Tech, but better for San Diego

The job: one ~8'0 to keep in San Diego so the Pickup Stick stops flying back and forth. At least Pickup Stick volume (59.1 L), never less, to protect wave count, with a lower entry rocker and narrower nose than the Lib Tech (no noserider features), more turnable than the Torq 8'2 V+ (~70 L), which stays in San Diego for knee-high days and guests. Mass-produced or a stock model, not a design-your-own custom. This is progress at 8'0, not a step down.

The San Diego pick — a touch more foam than the Pickup Stick, low entry for glide, a tail built to turn

64.0 L 8'0 × 22⅝ × 3.05 Quad ~$1,000 (est.) · made to order, 8–12 wks

  • Why #1 (Sep 25, 2026): it's the board to buy now and keep in San Diego. Matt likes its shape best, and Duffy likes Rusty.
  • Size (Sep 26): 8'0 (64.0 L), up from the 7'11 (61.5 L) — Matt still misses rolling San Diego waves on the Torq, so the extra foam protects wave count. The low entry rocker should also help it catch waves. Confirm the dimensions at the shop, since Rusty's chart has typos.

2 · Future step down

Step down on hold (Sep 24, 2026): progress at 8'0 first. Riding Patrick's HI Moe 7'2 (49.4 L) at Swami's — one wave caught, stood up on two more and fell — showed the sloppy habits the longer Torq forgives. The Lib Tech Pickup Stick 8'0 (59.1 L) is the right board for now. Plan (Patrick's suggestion): fix these at 8'0 first. In San Diego that means the #1 Lowrider 8'0 — a bit more foam than the Pickup Stick (64.0 vs 59.1 L) in a better San Diego shape, not a step down.

Ready to step down when, on the 8'0 on a normal 2–3 ft day:

  • Catch rate: about 70% of the waves you commit to (in position, paddling hard until you stand or it passes), held across 3 sessions. On the Torq, this is also the sign to move to the Lowrider 8'0.
  • Peak: most waves are taken at or near the peak, not the shoulder or inside.
  • Pop-up: low, one motion, same stance almost every time — including steep late takeoffs.
  • Turn and burn: committing early and catching set waves off a quick turn is routine, not a highlight.
  • Speed: can pump to make a faster section, not just ride in trim.
  • Turns: bottom turn and cutback, frontside and backside, keeping enough speed to reach the pocket again.

Signs the 8'0 is holding you back: it feels slow to turn or the nose catches in the pocket; you're catching waves way before everyone else and want to take off deeper. Benchmark test: every month or two, borrow Patrick's Moe 7'2 on a small, soft day — five waves caught and most of them made means you're ready (baseline Sep 24: one). Rough timing: 20–30 focused sessions — about 4–5 months at 2026's pace, so a first check around early 2027. Step size: drop about 4–6 L at a time. From 59.1 L that means ~53–55 L next: the Moe 7'4 (52.9 L), or a shorter Lowrider (7'7, 52.0 L · 7'8, 54.0 L) in the same shape as the 8'0.

The step-down brief: step down from 8'0 to ~7'4–7'6 without paying a paddle tax. Priorities that drove the ranking: glide and glass over swell power · paddling effort is a first-class cost · 2–4 ft is the happy zone · turtle-roll volumes (duck diving is a future goal — every board this size is a turtle-roll board for everyone) · the main progression goal: easier turning — as of Sep 2026 (~167 sessions, actively working on turns) this now outranks rail forgiveness: a board that turns from a normal stance, without stepping far back, beats one that's hard to bury but slow to come around · doesn't love the black basalt look — white finishes preferred · travels with his boards and doesn't want to deal with dings on the road — toughness is a stated priority.

Harley Ingleby Moe 7'4

The step-down pick — the right-size drop from 8'0, forgiving, and the toughest to fly with

52.9 L 7'4 × 22½ × 2⅞ 5 boxes — thruster or quad ~$1,210

Size run: 7'2 (49.4 L) · 7'4 (52.9 L) · 8'0 (61.2 L) — plus the separate Moe Mini model at 6'6 (41 L) and 6'10 (45 L).

  • Most volume of the four (52.9 L) — the smallest step down from the Pickup Stick, and reviewers call it the small-wave mid-length.
  • Rocker: flat through the middle (the paddle-and-glide engine) with a flicked-up nose that barely drags in trim. More curve than the Machados, but it's the small-wave-friendly kind — reviewers report early entry in knee-high mush — and the curve is what makes turning easier, which is the goal. Nothing like the Pickup Stick's continuous steep-wave rocker.
  • Thunderbolt construction is markedly more ding-resistant than Firewire's EPS builds — the best board here to fly with.
  • Flat bottom with double concave between your feet — exactly the bottom shape that makes carving accessible on a wide board. Harley's series exists to bring performance turning to mid-lengths.
  • Five FCS II boxes — thruster or quad (Harley recommends his XL quad set on the 7'4), so it's not locked into a funboard feel.
  • Rail forgiveness: rounded rails and a squash-style tail are also hard to bury — on par with the Seaside & Beyond for mistake-tolerance, and the most forgiving overall thanks to the extra volume.
  • Construction — Thunderbolt Red: EPS core with internal carbon flex beams instead of a stringer; the skin is vacuum-bagged in epoxy, curing with maximum cloth and minimum brittle resin. In the water that means light but damped, with tuned spring through turns; in the board bag it's the most ding-proof build here — the no-hassle-traveling pick.
  • Why #1 (Sep 24, 2026): after the Moe 7'2 session, the lesson is to step down gradually. At 52.9 L it's about a 6 L drop from the 59.1 L Pickup Stick, the smallest here; the others are 8.5–11 L drops, closer to the jump that humbled Matt on the 7'2. Rounded rails and the extra foam forgive the mistakes you still make on turns; Thunderbolt is the best build here to fly with; and it's the same shape as Patrick's 7'2, so the benchmark sessions carry over directly and Patrick can coach on it. The trade: a bit more rocker than the softest San Diego days want, which the 8'0 covers. A shorter Lowrider is the other step-down path if the 8'0 clicks.

Firewire Xtra Cado 7'6

The easiest-turning board here — a good second step or small-day board

50.6 L 7'6 × 21⅞ × 2¾ 5 boxes $1,125 at Surf Ride (white Helium) · 7'6 sold out direct

Size run: 6'6 (38.7 L) · 6'8 (39.3 L) · 7'0 (44.4 L) · 7'2 (47.1 L) · 7'6 (50.6 L).

  • Machado's stretched Machadocado: real glide for small, gutless waves, with more foam (50.6 L) than the Seaside & Beyond.
  • Pulled-down, thinner rails and double-concave-to-vee add turnability — the most "performance" of the mellow options.
  • Tail: a narrower, rounded tail with vee — looser than the Seaside & Beyond's wide swallow and keel-quad hold. Per the Surf Ride salesperson (and consistent with the shape), it turns without having to step your back foot as far back — the direct fix for Matt's logged habit of turning with too much weight forward and bogging the rail (Sep 15, 2026).
  • Rail trade-off: the pulled-down rails are the easiest here to put on edge — and the easiest to accidentally bury when weight or timing is off. At this stage that's accepted: they give the best turn feedback of the four, which is what accelerates turn progression.
  • Rocker: low, Machado-school flat with vee out the tail — nearly the Seaside & Beyond's glide, and the vee rolls the board rail-to-rail with the least effort of the four.
  • Construction — Helium core + fiberglass: the same wood-rail stringerless EPS core as the Seaside & Beyond, but skinned in standard fiberglass instead of basalt. The lightest, flexiest feel of the four — and the least reinforced skin, so the most ding-prone: the weakest fit for the no-ding-hassle-while-traveling requirement. Travel it in a padded bag.
  • Why #2 (was the overall pick on Sep 23): still the easiest rail-to-rail turning of the four (vee out a narrower tail), built for the soft 2–4 ft waves Matt surfs. But it's a ~8.5 L drop from the 8'0, and Patrick's critique holds: it spreads its foam across a wide outline with thin, pulled-down rails, so there's little foam at the rail when you're a bit off balance. After the 7'2 session, that's a later step, not the first one. White Helium one at Surf Ride, $1,125.

Firewire Seaside & Beyond 7'4

The glide machine — best soft-wave glide, and there's a white version

49.5 L 7'4 × 21¾ × 2¾ Quad ~$1,105 white Helium (2024 run) · $1,255 Volcanic

Size run: 6'8 (40.9 L) · 7'0 (45.3 L) · 7'4 (49.5 L) — current Volcanic production; older LFT/Helium runs included other lengths (e.g. the used-market 7'6).

  • Flattest entry rocker of the four — the direct fix for what the steep-rockered Pickup Stick lacks in soft San Diego walls.
  • Designed by Rob Machado for 1–5 ft lined-up Cardiff-style surf: your 2–4 ft happy zone is its sweet spot.
  • Quad fish DNA means down-the-line speed without pumping — glide and glass, not swell power.
  • 49.5 L keeps the wave count high, and a 7'4 is easier to turtle roll than your 8'0.
  • Half a foot shorter and much less swing weight than the 8'0 — noticeably easier to turn, though the fish outline favors drawn-out flowing arcs over tighter rail-to-rail work.
  • Rail forgiveness (the turn-progression priority): full, fishy rails engage gradually and are hard to accidentally bury — turns initiate with body lean rather than a knife-edge catching. The trade is muted feedback, which is the right trade at this stage.
  • Finish — white is an older run, not current: Helium itself is still in production (it's what the current Xtra Cado is), but Firewire's site now offers this model only in Volcanic (black basalt) — no reason given. The white board pictured is the Helium + standard fiberglass build (Moment Surf Co listed it Sep 2024, ~$1,105) — shop stock from the previous production run, same 7'4 × 21¾ × 2¾ / 49.4 L shape. Both Moment and South Coast show it sold out, so it's a hunt: call SD shops or check the used market.
  • Construction — Helium core + Volcanic lamination (current) or fiberglass (white, older run): stringerless EPS with paulownia wood rails doing the flex work, wrapped in basalt cloth — actual volcanic-rock fiber, stronger than fiberglass and more elastic than carbon (and the source of the black look). Very light and springy; still hand-laminated EPS, so it needs more ding care than Thunderbolt. The white fiberglass version is as ding-prone as the Xtra Cado.
  • Why #3: still the best small-wave glide here with gentle, full rails — but at 49.5 L it's a ~9.6 L drop from the 8'0, the biggest step of the three soft-wave boards, and the swallow/keel-quad tail favors long arcs over the turning Matt is working on. New ones only come in black basalt; the white Helium version is a sold-out 2024 run.

How the ranking was made

Weighted to how you actually surf, not spec-sheet maximalism:

  1. Wave match first. The target is soft, less-steep San Diego surf in your 2–4 ft comfort band. The two Machado shapes and the Moe are built for exactly that; the TJ Pro Mid prefers a touch more energy.
  2. Paddle effort is a first-class cost. Nothing under 48 L made the list. The Moe (52.9 L) is the paddle king; the Seaside & Beyond gives up little while shedding half a foot of length.
  3. Easier turning is the goal — and progression is reps × design. Every board here turns far easier than the 8'0 (less length, less swing weight). Beyond that, turn progression comes from turns attempted, which favors the board that catches the most waves — and from turn-friendly design: vee or double-concave bottoms and responsive flex that make getting on rail easy. The ranking weighs both.
  4. Step down gradually (revised Sep 24, 2026). Turning is still the goal, but the Moe 7'2 session showed a big drop in volume exposes every sloppy habit at once. Size of the step now ranks first: ~3–6 L at a time, with rails forgiving enough to learn turns on.
  5. Channel Islands CI Mid as a step-down: left off the ranking above. The 7'6 (51.8 L) is the right volume, but CI pitches it for shoulder-high to nearly double-overhead surf with hard, tucked rails, and all 11 of Matt's observed-size sessions were 2.5–4 ft faces. It's #2 for the San Diego board, at 7'10.
  6. Also considered for the San Diego board (all near Lib Tech volume): Lost Smooth Operator 8'0 (59 L, very low entry, 4+1, often sold out), Pyzel Mid Length Crisis 7'10 (58.4 L, $1,195, in stock, most performance-oriented), Stewart 2FUN 7'10 (59 L, clear finishes, more cruiser), Rusty Egg 7'9 (59.6 L, knee-high up, more forgiving), Rusty Express 7'10 (60.2 L, twin/single pin — Matt preferred the Lowrider), a second Pickup Stick (wrong nose and rocker for San Diego). Christenson was skipped (mostly custom; the stock C-Bucket is a trim single fin).
  7. It has to survive the trip. Matt travels with his boards and explicitly doesn't want ding hassle on the road — a dinged EPS board mid-trip means finding a repair shop or sitting out sessions. Thunderbolt > Firewire EPS here, the Moe's strongest card. A day bag is still worth it.

Final call (Sep 25, 2026). San Diego: buy the Rusty Lowrider 8'0 now (then Moe 8'0, then CI Mid 7'10). It's a bit more foam than the Pickup Stick (64.0 vs 59.1 L) with a lower entry rocker, a narrower nose and a tail built to turn, so Matt keeps progressing at 8'0 without flying boards. Future step down: once the checklist is done, Moe 7'4 → Xtra Cado 7'6 → Seaside & Beyond 7'4 → TJ Pro Mid 7'4, or a shorter Lowrider in the same shape. History: Aug 8 Seaside & Beyond (rail forgiveness); Sep 23 Xtra Cado (turning); Sep 24 Moe 7'4 (step size); Sep 25 Lowrider 7'11 for San Diego (no lower than the Lib Tech's volume); Sep 26 Lowrider 8'0 (still missing waves on the Torq). If a demo says otherwise, trust the demo.
