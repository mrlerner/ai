Kaua‘i, First Week of June

The brief: a concentrated tropical week, not a quasi-living surf stay — San Diego is for that. Seven nights in one place on the North Shore, three or four surf mornings chosen each dawn rather than booked ahead, Nā Pali by small boat as the centerpiece, one big hike, and the rest left unscheduled. Early June, so the trip is done before the Pacific Northwest’s July–September window opens.

Target dates: roughly June 3–10 or June 6–13, 2027. Saturday to Saturday, nonstop both ways.

THE RIDER, HONESTLY

Comfortable to about 5-foot faces, still building turns, happiest on forgiving, shouldery waves. Hanalei in June is that wave when it exists at all; Kalapaki and the South Shore are the backups when the North Shore goes flat. The one coaching session on the list is not a stand-up lesson — it is someone who picks the right break for the day and explains Kaua‘i’s reefs, currents, positioning and etiquette.

Read this first · Hurricane Lowell, September 2026

Lowell crossed Kaua‘i on the night of September 6–7, 2026, with roughly 90 mph winds. It knocked out power to 33,000 of the island’s 36,000 utility customers and did the worst damage on the South Shore: storm surge through the first floor of the Sheraton, along Lawai Road and in front of Brennecke’s at Po‘ipū. As of mid-September every state park on the island is closed until further notice, including Hā‘ena, Kē‘ē, the Kalalau Trail and the Nā Pali coast; the Hā‘ena shuttle is suspended; Hanalei Beach Park near Black Pot is closed; Nā Pali Catamaran is dark until at least September 19; and the main North Shore rental agency is not accepting arrivals before October 1. Some remote park experiences may not reopen until 2027.

For a June 2027 trip that is nine months of recovery, which is probably enough — but it moves three things onto the must-confirm list before booking anything non-refundable: that Hā‘ena State Park and its reservation system are open, that the Hanalei-side boat operators are running, and that Po‘ipū Beach Park and its beach infrastructure are back. Sources: Civil Beat, County of Kaua‘i, Go Hā‘ena, Parrish Kaua‘i update.

Surf strategy

Plan A · Hanalei

Summer Hanalei is a different bay from winter: south wraps and afternoon trade windswell instead of North Pacific power. Pine Trees (Wai‘oli) is the consistent, gentle longboard peak where the bay’s surfers actually paddle out; Waikoko at the west end is reef-sheltered and gentler again. Sandy bottom. The honest risk is flat, not big — early June can go days with nothing rideable, which is why the week never locks the break in advance.

Plan B · Kalapaki

A protected cove in front of Līhu‘e that stays clean almost year-round. The place for a progression-focused session: paddling, positioning, turns on a forgiving wave. Note that the on-beach surf school suspends lessons from mid-June to mid-August, so rent a board on the North Shore and bring it.

Plan C · the South Shore

From May to October south swells light up Po‘ipū: Lemon Drops is the slow beginner peak, too slow for the locals and perfect for this brief. Best combined with the Po‘ipū snorkel afternoon. This is the shore Lowell hit hardest — confirm the beach park is open before building a day around it.

The coaching session

Book one private, early in the week, with a school that coaches intermediates rather than teaching pop-ups: Hanalei Surf School (founded by pro Evan Valiere; 90-minute privates on wave reading and positioning) or Aloha Surfing School’s 60-minute elite coaching at Hanalei, about $240 solo. The ask is Kaua‘i-specific reef, current and etiquette knowledge, plus a read on where to surf the rest of the week.

Water and gear

78–79°F in June. Boardshorts and a rash guard; reef booties are a debate at Po‘ipū, not at Hanalei. Rent a longer board than pride suggests — the June waves are small and the whole point is time on the wave, not the wave itself.

Nā Pali · the centerpiece

Why summer
Smaller-raft and catamaran operators run their full sea-cave-and-snorkel itinerary May–September because winter swell closes the caves and the landings. June is squarely inside the window.
From Hanalei
Nā Pali Catamaran runs 35-ft cats from Hanalei Bay, 15 minutes from the coast, 4-hour snorkel-and-sightseeing tours, small enough to tuck into the caves, ages 4+. No full day lost to transit. They cancel when the bay is unsafe, so book Monday and keep Friday open as the fallback.
From the west side
Capt. Andy’s and Nā Pali Riders run rigid-hull rafts from Kikiaola / Port Allen: about 6 hours, $285–295 per adult, a beach landing at Nū‘alolo Kai with a walk through the 800-year-old village site and a snorkel with a good chance of monk seals. Bouncier ride, bigger day, and a 1 h 20–2 h drive from Princeville each way.
The call
Hanalei-side boat if it is running in 2027 and everyone is fine with a modest boat in open water; the west-side raft if the Nū‘alolo Kai landing matters more than the drive. Either way: Monday, so a cancellation has four days to reschedule into.

Hiking and Hā‘ena

Awa‘awapuhi
6.4 mi round trip, about 1,900 ft, 4–4.5 h. It descends from 4,120 ft to the Nū‘alolo valley rim at 2,500 ft, so the climb is on the way home. The lookout is the Nā Pali from above, the mirror of Monday’s boat. Start early; the ridge clouds over by early afternoon.
Hā‘ena reservations
Entry is by reservation only through gohaena.com, released 30 days ahead at midnight Hawai‘i time, and they sell out at once; cancellations reappear around 12:15 am and again 7–8 am. Options: the shuttle from Waipā or Princeville ($45 adult, entry included) or parking plus entry ($20 per car per slot plus $5 per person) in three time slots. For a Tuesday visit, set an alarm for the Monday 30 days out.
Kalalau, the short version
Kē‘ē to Hanakāpī‘ai Beach is 2 miles each way with the coast views the trail is famous for; no permit is needed to that point, and anything past the valley requires a camping permit even for day hikers. Do not swim at Hanakāpī‘ai.
Tunnels, honestly
The inner lagoon at Makua (Tunnels) is now a pu‘uhonua fish refuge and snorkeling there is prohibited. Kē‘ē and ‘Anini are the North Shore snorkels for this trip.

Book ahead

The checklist, in order
  • Flights. Alaska’s SEA–LIH nonstop runs twice daily, about 6 h 15 out. Saturday-to-Saturday for the June 5 or June 12 pattern.
  • Lodging. Seven nights, Hanalei or Princeville, one place. Book early; June is high season and post-Lowell inventory may be thinner.
  • Rental car. Non-negotiable on this island.
  • Nā Pali boat. Monday; confirm the operator is running post-Lowell; keep Friday as the reschedule.
  • Hā‘ena / Kē‘ē. Exactly 30 days before the Tuesday, midnight HST. Confirm the park has reopened first.
  • Coaching. One private session Sunday or Tuesday morning.
  • Board. Reserve a rental in Hanalei town for the week rather than trusting beach concessions.

June vs September

WHY JUNE WINS · The North Shore is at its most approachable, the Nā Pali boats have their best access of the year, south swells are already running for the Po‘ipū backup, and temperatures are a notch below late summer. Early June also lands before the school-vacation crowds and, most importantly, leaves July–September untouched for the Pacific Northwest.

THE COST · Hanalei can go flat. September brings more consistent North Shore waves, but it spends the best PNW month and its hurricane exposure is what 2026 just demonstrated. The week above absorbs the June risk by staying flexible and by having Kalapaki and the South Shore in reserve.

THE STRATEGY, RESTATED · San Diego for the long, quasi-living surf stays. Kaua‘i for the concentrated tropical week, in early June, so the summer stays home.
