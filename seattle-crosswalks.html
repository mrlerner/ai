How it works

Using the map

Pinch or use two fingers to zoom and pan. Zoomed out, the map shows every corner in Seattle that needs a crosswalk, with the city's 10 riskiest numbered. Zoom in on a neighborhood and tap the chip at the bottom to open its page, where its corners are numbered by risk and existing crosswalks show in green. Tap any numbered pin to open that corner.

How we rate risk

Each corner on SDOT's list gets a Risk Score from 0 to 100: where it ranks among all of SDOT's priority corners in Seattle. A 72 means riskier than 72% of them. Highest risk is 90–100, Very risky 70–89, Somewhat risky 50–69 and Lower risk below 50. We leave out about 40 spots on SDOT's list where every street is a highway, ramp, tunnel or bridge deck, since nobody crosses there on foot.

The score is how many pedestrian crashes to expect at the corner each year, so it counts both how dangerous the crossing is and how many people are there to be hurt. A wide, busy road with nobody walking scores lower than one where people cross all day. We estimate it the way traffic engineers do when crashes are rare, following the federal Highway Safety Manual. First, a model predicts crashes from how much traffic passes (SDOT's 2024 counts), how many people come to the area (jobs within a quarter mile from the Census, and nearby schools, transit, parks and shops), and how many lanes people cross. Its weights come from Seattle's own pedestrian crashes since 2016 at the corner or on the blocks next to it. Then each corner's own crash record is blended in. One crash is often bad luck, so it moves a corner a little; a corner with crash after crash moves a lot.

We checked it the honest way: built only from crashes before 2021, it picked out the corners where pedestrians were hit afterward better than SDOT's priority score, a plain crash count, or our earlier score. The 10% of corners it rated riskiest had about half of all pedestrian crashes since 2021. Posted speed, residents nearby and distance to the nearest crosswalk didn't improve those predictions, so they're shown on each corner but not scored.

It's an estimate, not a measurement. The city doesn't count pedestrians at most corners, and crash records only include crashes reported to police, so a corner with no crashes on record can still be dangerous. Counting people who cross, and adding notes about near-misses, helps fill that gap. Votes show what neighbors want most; they don't change the Risk Score.

SDOT priority

SDOT gives every corner on its list its own score, which we call SDOT priority so it isn't confused with the Risk Score. Points come from places people walk to nearby (schools, parks, transit and busy blocks, up to 3 points each), safety (how dangerous the street is to cross, up to 16) and equity (up to 12 for neighborhoods SDOT's equity index flags as underserved). The highest score in Seattle is 37.

Scheduled crosswalks

A corner is scheduled once SDOT opens a work order for a crosswalk there. SDOT paints crosswalks in the dry months, about April to September, usually a few months or more after opening the work order. Some take a year or longer.

Data

Priority corners and scores come from SDOT's Enhanced Street Crossings priority network (Seattle Transportation Plan). Crosswalks, plans and build years come from SDOT's Marked Crosswalks data and traffic work orders. Pedestrian crashes come from SDOT's collision records, traffic from SDOT's 2024 arterial traffic counts, and jobs from the Census's LEHD Origin-Destination Employment Statistics. Neighborhood boundaries come from Seattle's Neighborhood Map Atlas. Votes, flag kits and neighbor activity are sample data.

About

Matt wanted a crosswalk near his house so his parents could feel safe crossing the street, especially in a Seattle winter, when it's dark after dinner. He realized it wasn't clear how to get a crosswalk built, how to get crossing flags, or how to organize his neighbors, so he built Seattle Crosswalk Watch.
