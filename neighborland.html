Neighborland (neighborland.com) began as a site where neighbors finished the sentence "I want ___ in my neighborhood" and backed each other's ideas with one tap. It became paid engagement software for city agencies, and Nextdoor bought it in 2019. It's the model our earlier competitive research says Crosswalk Watch should follow for engagement. This is the deep dive: what it looked like, who built it, how it got people and kept them, how much traffic it really had, and why it worked.

Researched October 2026 from the Internet Archive, Neighborland's own Handbook blog, a 2015 Neighborland sales deck, co-founder Candy Chang's site, the SEC and press. Most numbers are the company's own and are marked "company-reported". Screenshots are from the Internet Archive unless the caption says otherwise.

The short version

As a consumer website, Neighborland was small. About a year in, only a few hundred people in New Orleans were using it. Its Twitter following leveled off around 4,600. Alexa ranked the site outside the top 900,000 in the world, which suggests roughly 10,000 to 30,000 visitors a month. The "3 million people" figure counts everyone who took part in client projects, including in person. It isn't website traffic.

What worked was the system around the site:

  1. It started on the street. People wrote on stickers, giant boards and tear-off flyers at farmers' markets, festivals and bus stops. Volunteers typed the answers in afterward. The website was where the input landed and the conversation continued.
  2. One simple question with a one-tap answer. Finishing "I want ___" took ten seconds, and "Me Too" took one tap. Each idea page led with its count: "43 neighbors want…".
  3. Partners brought their own crowds. Food truck coalitions, bike groups, a land trust and San Francisco's planning department each pointed their members at a page with one question on it.
  4. The best results came when someone had promised to listen. The ideas that got built had an official on the other end: a city bike coordinator, a planning department with a plan due. Neighborland's business became selling that promise to agencies that had a decision coming.
  5. It followed up. "Me Too" also subscribed you to updates. Staff posted progress in the thread, and emails went out at key points in a project. Ideas that happened were marked "Made it Happen".

What didn't work: wishes with nobody to answer them, a small and unrepresentative audience, participation counts that mostly counted page visitors, and a free consumer product with no revenue model. Nextdoor's purchase was a good exit for a modest business, not a sign of a big one.

Founders and team

  • Dan Parham, CEO and head of product. A designer, earlier creative director at Time Warner and director of user experience at Yahoo. Press cast him as the businessman to Chang's dreamer. He personally led the company's 200 or so partnerships. At Nextdoor he ran the public agency team, about 20 people serving more than 4,000 agencies, including through COVID. Since August 2025 he has been head of state and local government at OpenAI (about.me).
  • Tee Parham, CTO. An engineer who ran a software consultancy in Boulder and led RGeo, an open-source Ruby mapping library. He led a Nextdoor engineering team in Colorado after the deal and is now at Workday.
  • Candy Chang, co-founder. An urban planner, artist and TED Senior Fellow whose sticker project started it all; she later made "Before I Die" walls. By 2016 she was listed as co-founder and advisor. No source says when or why she stepped back; her art career took off at the same time.
  • The team stayed tiny. In 2015 it was the three founders, two engineers, a facilitation lead, a sales lead, and about 100 freelance local facilitators who ran events. Alan Williams, the New Orleans community manager, was its first person on the ground.

Money

  • Grants first. Tulane's Social Entrepreneurship Program and the Rockefeller Foundation paid for the 2011 New Orleans pilot; no amounts were published.
  • One seed round, never another. The SEC Form D shows $1,195,005 from 7 investors in April 2012 (SEC). The investors were True Ventures, Evan Williams, Biz Stone and Jason Goldman of Obvious Corporation, Lerer Ventures, SV Angel and New Orleans' Voodoo Ventures (TechCrunch). No later round was found.
  • No revenue model for the first two years. In 2011 Parham said cities wouldn't be charged. The plan was to sell developers pages that asked "what do you want at this building?", and maybe sell neighborhood data. In 2012 the company was putting money off, Twitter-style, while paying a community manager in every new city (Next City).
  • Then it charged organizations by the month (company-reported):
Monthly price DIY Basic Partner
November 2016 $500 $1,000 $1,500+
January 2019 $1,000 $2,000 $2,500+

On top of that came paid services: program design, workshops, facilitator training, moderation and reports. Small towns paid a sliding scale of about $0.03 per resident per month. Residents always used it free, and it never ran ads. Contract sizes and revenue were never published. At the 2019 prices, a couple hundred organizations over eight years suggests a business of a few million dollars a year at most.

  • The exit. Nextdoor bought it in late 2019 to build out its tools for public agencies; terms weren't disclosed (GovTech). Nextdoor's agency tools are free to agencies. Neighborland kept serving existing partners while its tools were built into Nextdoor, and the platform shut down in 2023.

How it looked and worked

2010–2012: "I want ___ in my neighborhood"

It started with paper. Candy Chang's stickers, then Neighborland's own, put the question on the building or street it was about.

The website rolled out city by city. Each new city started with a waitlist: "Enter your email and city and we'll let you know when we arrive."

A city page was a list of wishes ranked by support. The count was the headline.

2013–2014: everywhere, then organizations

In December 2012 any US city could sign up. The homepage became the sentence itself, with a resident wearing her sticker.

Open-ended wishes spread thin across hundreds of cities with no one to answer them. So organizations were given "Questions" to put to residents: what do you want at this building, on this corridor, in this plaza? A question gave each campaign a topic, a deadline and an owner who had promised to read the answers.

2015–2019: software for agencies

By 2015 the homepage was a sales page.

The street toolkit became part of the product. Organizers set up whiteboards and booths where foot traffic already was, and volunteers typed the input in.

How it got people

Residents

There's no breakdown of signups by channel. By the company's own account, people came through these routes, roughly in order of importance:

  1. In person, where foot traffic already was. Its 2014 guide to engaging the public offers three tools: stickers, posters and mobile whiteboards. Its advice is to go to farmers' markets and festivals and not expect people to come to you. Print the web address and text-in number on the sticker's back, keep a clipboard for names and emails, and post stickers no higher than 30 inches so kids and wheelchair users can reach them. Afterward, organizers bulk-uploaded the ideas, "me toos" and emails, so offline people counted the same as online ones.
  2. Partner organizations bringing their members. Each city launched with partners: community groups, economic development groups or the city. In New Orleans, Bike Easy, Transport for NOLA and the Food Truck Coalition each sponsored a two-month challenge. Memphis launched at a party co-hosted with the nonprofit ioby. In Oakland there were listening sessions at four libraries, with posters left behind.
  3. Text and tweet to post. Billboards, bus-stop signs and flyers listed a phone number to text ideas to. A Twitter bot turned any tweet starting "I want ___" into an idea and replied with its link.
  4. Social sharing by the agency. For Dix Park, 67% of referrals came from Facebook and 26% from Twitter. In Mesa, the mayor and council shared the project from their own accounts.
  5. Press as credibility. TED, Fast Company, NPR, GigaOm, and Business Insider's "20 Most Inspiring Companies of 2012". This helped win investors and agencies more than residents.

Paid ads barely appear: a 2012 job posting asked for AdWords and Facebook Ads skills, and that's all.

Agencies

  • Land and expand in San Francisco. It started with a 2012 corner beautification in one neighborhood, which led the district supervisor to give $15,000. Then a safer 6th Street with the transit agency in 2013, the Market Street Prototyping Festival in 2014, and formal planning department plans in 2015. By 2016 Neighborland was building features for SF Planning and putting them in the core product. It claimed 117,000 San Francisco participants.
  • Riding along with consultants. It joined project teams led by planning firms (Perkins+Will, Gehl, HNTB, Street Plans and others). Big wins like Turner Field in Atlanta and Dix Park came through those firms.
  • Case studies with famous names. The homepage and deck led with testimonials from Twitter, the Rockefeller Foundation and Greenbelt Alliance, plus partner logos like Google.
  • A promise agencies could repeat: 10 to 100 times the participation of traditional outreach at a tenth of the cost, and every plan it supported approved (company-reported).

No evidence was found of conference booths, cold sales or RFP-driven growth; Raleigh's Dix Park is the one known RFP win.

How it kept them

  • One tap to take part, and the count as the headline. "Me Too" was a like, a vote and a subscription in one. Pages said "396 neighbors want…", so every tap visibly moved the number.
  • Comments could be backed too. A comment could show "5 neighbors support", so even replies were a way to agree.
  • A reward for crossing a threshold. In 2011, an idea with 100 votes got a sketch from the design team and help getting it in front of the right people. Staff also picked popular ideas (a supermarket, the St. Claude streetcar) and worked on them directly.
  • Answering "what's next?" At a June 2011 user-research session, people mostly checked their own ideas and who liked their comments. Their biggest question was "What's next?" That led to the Handbook, a how-to guide for making ideas happen, and to notifications after a "Me Too". On a 2011 streetcar idea a neighbor asked, "What is the current status on this? Anybody care to give an update?"
  • Updates in the thread. Staff posted news, letters and status in the comments of an idea. Later, "Made it Happen" flags and a public Progress page showed the wins.
  • Email at key moments. Residents chose real-time, daily or weekly email. Organizers could message an idea's supporters, and notifications went out when a meeting was coming or the input period closed. Mesa sent more than 30,000 and Dix Park more than 100,000.
  • Something to do besides vote. In 2013–14 every idea page had a "How can you help?" box to add a resource, event, petition or fundraiser.
  • Named listeners. In agency projects, "Partners" were officials who said they read the discussion. The Dix Park page told residents the way to get their attention was to answer questions and get their network to "Me Too".

How to read the gap. The big totals are cumulative and come from a few large projects. Market Street alone claimed 250,000, mostly in-person festival visitors. They count visitors, not people who took part. In Mesa, the city's own numbers were 65,000+ site visits and about 10,000 people who posted, commented or supported. A local blogger estimated only about 1 in 6 visitors did anything. Dix Park reported 10,000 direct participants in 2018 and later claimed 67,000. A fair summary: tens of thousands of people actually posted or supported something in a good year, and most of them came from a few agency projects with big promotional pushes.

Why it worked, and what didn't

Why it worked:

  • The question was the product. "I want ___ in my neighborhood" is easy to answer, works the same on a sticker, a whiteboard, a text or a web form, and says something about the person who answers. Wearing or posting a sticker is public, and that brings in the next person.
  • Physical first, digital second. Most participants started on the street. The site's job was to collect everything in one place, count it and keep talking. That's why it reached people who would never visit a civic website.
  • Partners did the acquisition. Neighborland never built a mass audience of its own. Each campaign borrowed one: a coalition's members, a city's social media accounts, a farmers' market crowd. When it worked, it made those partners look good.
  • An owner made it count. Wins came when someone with power had promised to read it: a bike coordinator, a supervisor, a planning department with a plan to approve. The Houston idea moved with 8 supporters, while a Baltimore request for a new bus route drew 27 and had no one to answer it. Selling that promise to agencies, rather than hoping for it, is what made it a business.
  • Counts and updates gave people a reason to come back. The supporter count as the headline, "Me Too" as a subscription, and staff updates in the thread answered the question users asked most: what's next?

What didn't work:

  • Open wishes with no owner. Most ideas drew a handful of supporters and went nowhere. Its biggest early push, about 2,500 signatures for the St. Claude streetcar, lost its federal grant. The company later called the effort naive. The line opened in 2016 on city money.
  • The audience was narrow. NPR found early users were mostly white, educated professionals. Some residents dominated agency discussions until the tool started truncating long comments and merging repeats.
  • The numbers were inflated. Counting page visitors as "participants" made the totals hard to trust.
  • Campaigns outgrew it. The New Orleans Food Truck Coalition, one of its best success stories, eventually needed organizing tools Neighborland didn't have.
  • No consumer business. With no ads and nothing charged to residents, the only money was agency contracts, so the product followed the agencies' projects rather than neighbors' everyday wishes.

What Crosswalk Watch should take from it

Our earlier competitive research already lists the main lessons in order: real votes, a fill-in-the-blank reason, putting the question on the corner, closing the loop, getting someone to promise to read it, working through existing groups, reaching people offline, and deciding who pays. This research adds these, roughly in order of payoff:

  1. Make the count the headline. "12 neighbors want a crosswalk at Latona Ave NE & NE 47th St." should be the corner page's title, the share text, the flyer's headline and the subject of the council summary, so each vote visibly moves it.
  2. Make a vote a subscription, and say so. Neighborland's "Me Too" was also "keep me updated", which is what kept people around. The vote box should say plainly that voters hear when SDOT's status for the corner changes, and the automatic updates from the data rebuild should go out.
  3. A tear-off flyer for each corner's pole. The LaSalle flyer is Crosswalk Watch's corner sign almost exactly: the question at the top, a web address on each tab. Print each corner's question, vote count and QR code, with tabs that have the corner's short link.
  4. A reward for reaching a threshold. Neighborland's 2011 rule was 100 votes and the design team sketches your idea and gets it in front of the right people. A Crosswalk Watch version: at N votes in a neighborhood, the corner goes into the council member's monthly summary with a ready-to-send request. Show the progress toward it on the page.
  5. Input typed in later counts too. Allow a neighbor captain to enter votes and reasons collected on paper at a farmers' market or PTA meeting, marked as collected in person. That's how Neighborland reached people who weren't online.
  6. A text-in number. Billboards and bus-stop signs with a text number brought in people who would never type a URL. "Text LATONA47 to …" on the pole sign is the same idea.
  7. Show the wins. Neighborland's Progress page and "Made it Happen" flags were the proof that taking part mattered. A "built" list on the neighborhood and city pages, from SDOT's own records, does that better than Neighborland could, since Crosswalk Watch doesn't have to claim credit to show results.
  8. Count honestly. Report voters and confirmed votes, never page visitors. Credibility with SDOT and council offices is the whole strategy, and Neighborland's inflated totals are a warning.
  9. Win one city, then sell what it needed. Neighborland's growth went through San Francisco Planning, building what it needed and making that the product. For taking the site to other cities, the equivalent is to get SDOT or a council office to actually use the corner data, then offer that to the next city.
