This week in Seattle
Nature & sky · free · timing correction
The crescent moon passes Venus Sunday and Monday — but the window is 7:30 to 7:50, not a quarter to eight, and Monday is the night to take
SUN SEP 13 & MON SEP 14, 7:30–7:50PM  ·  FREE  ·  ANY OPEN WESTERN HORIZON
Correcting this brief for three mornings running: it has been printing “about 7:55.” Positions computed this morning from the Jet Propulsion Laboratory's ephemeris service, for this latitude and longitude, say that is too late. By 7:55 on Sunday, Venus is 2.5° up and the moon is 0.8° up — that is the rooftops, not the sky. Sunset is 7:27pm Sunday and 7:25pm Monday. The show is the twenty minutes after.
Sunday at 7:35pm: Venus sits 5.4° above the west-southwest horizon — about three fingers at arm's length — at magnitude −4.75, which is bright enough to find in the leftover daylight. The moon is a 9.9% crescent 4.8° to Venus's lower right, and lower down, so it is the harder of the two to catch. Both are on the horizon by 8:05.
Monday at 7:35pm, and this is the better night: the moon has crossed over. It is now a 16.7% crescent 7.3° to Venus's upper left — wider apart, but the moon is the higher of the pair at 6.2° and thicker, so it is easier to see and it lingers after Venus drops. Monday night is forecast 0% cloud against Sunday's 5%. If you only get one, get Monday.
Where to stand: it happens low, so it needs water — Golden Gardens, Carkeek, Alki, Discovery Park's Sound side. Fifteen minutes and you are home by eight-thirty. There is no indoor fallback for this one, only the next clear evening, and Venus is sinking toward the sun from here; it hits its brightest of the year on the 22nd and is harder to see every week after. Worth knowing while you are out there: Venus is itself a 29%-lit crescent right now, which is exactly why it is so bright.
Water · free · the run is accelerating
Coho at the Ballard Locks have added 700 fish in three counted days — and Thursday is 76° and cloudless
SEASON TOTAL 2,315 THROUGH SEP 9  ·  FREE, OPEN DAILY  ·  HIRAM M. CHITTENDEN LOCKS, BALLARD
Yesterday this brief reported the coho total at 1,612 through September 6. The Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife has since posted three more days: the season total is now 2,315 through September 9, with 238 fish on Tuesday alone. Chinook are at 12,394, and the sockeye run has finished at 31,302 — that number has not moved since the start of the month. Counts are marked preliminary and post in clumps, so the real total today is higher than the page says.
Why it matters this week rather than any week: the coho peak is the last two weeks of September, so the run is climbing into it. The fish ladder has a below-water viewing gallery where the fish stack up against the glass at the fourteenth weir; it is free, open daily, and takes about twenty minutes to do properly.
Weather call: not Saturday, in 64% rain. Thursday is 76° and 0% — the warmest and clearest day in the eight-day file, and the locks plus the botanical garden and the Ballard side of the ship canal is an easy hour and a half. The indoor fallback is the site itself: the viewing gallery is underground and dry, so weather only spoils the walk in.
Music & culture · free · new this morning
El Grito turns ten — four free hours of orchestra, mariachi and Aztec dance, indoors, on the afternoon it is forecast to rain
SUN SEP 13, 1–5PM  ·  FREE  ·  THE GREAT HALL, TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
The tenth annual Seattle celebration of Mexican independence, and it is free, ticketed only so they can count heads. 1pm opens with a community fair and the first performances — Aztec dancers CAtl Tonalli and pianist Denisse Avena Arevalo. 2pm is the main concert: Paula Madrigal conducting the Ballard Civic Orchestra with the tenor José Rubio, then the Grito itself, the independence cry, called by the Mexican Consul. 4pm turns into a fiesta with Mariachi Hermanas Rosario, the folk dance company Bailadores de Bronce, Semilla Folklórica and Trío Guadalevín.
Weather call: Sunday is 68° with a 40% chance of rain before 5pm — which is exactly the window this occupies, and it is entirely indoors. Then it ends at 5, the sky clears, and you have two and a half hours before Venus. That is a whole Sunday that works in either direction, which is not something this weekend offers twice.
Free, all ages, and the orchestra half is a real concert rather than background music. Collect the free ticket on the event page above so they can plan the room.
Music & food · free · the other sunday option
Live Aloha Hawaiian Cultural Festival — seven free hours, two stages, and a covered building to retreat into
SUN SEP 13, 11AM–6PM  ·  FREE  ·  SEATTLE CENTER (ARMORY, MURAL AMPHITHEATRE, FISHER ROOFTOP)
One of the free Seattle Center Festál cultural festivals, put on by the community it belongs to rather than staged for visitors. Two stages of Hawaiian music and hula run the full seven hours, with a food hall's worth of Hawaiian cooking in the Armory, a marketplace, lei-making and other hands-on workshops, and a children's corner. Costs nothing, takes as long as you choose to give it, and is finished before dinner.
Weather call: same 40%-before-5pm Sunday. The indoor fallback is built into the site — the Armory Food and Event Hall is fully covered and the Fisher Pavilion rooftop is sheltered, so a shower moves the festival indoors rather than ending it. The open-air Mural Amphitheatre is the part at risk.
Choosing between the two Sunday free things: Live Aloha is the one to wander through with no plan; El Grito is the one to sit down for. They overlap from 1pm, and they are a fifteen-minute drive apart. Doing the festival in the late morning and the orchestra at two is a real option.
Ideas & cities · tuesday · $35
CityNerd walks through an unreleased video on Puget Sound light rail, live, with the region's best transit reporter arguing back
TUE SEP 15, 7–8:30PM  ·  $35  ·  THE GREAT HALL, TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
The closest thing this week to work you would do for fun, and it is still on the calendar with the same details as when this brief confirmed them. $35 general admission, 7pm to 8:30pm — a printed end time, and the earliest finish of any ticketed talk this week. There is a $65 pre-reception at 5:45; the $35 seat gets the same conversation.
Weather note: Tuesday is 70° and 0%. Spend the afternoon outdoors and the evening in here, not the reverse.
Nature learning · wednesday · $10
The naturalist who explained Puget Sound to this city does the Cascades next — $10, 6:30, free if you are a member
WED SEP 16, 6:30PM  ·  $10, FREE FOR MEMBERS, REGISTRATION REQUIRED  ·  BURKE MUSEUM, UNIVERSITY OF WASHINGTON CAMPUS
David B. Williams wrote Homewaters, the natural and human history of Puget Sound that most people around here have either read or been handed. In the Range of Fire and Ice does the same job on the mountains: Indigenous huckleberry grounds and the footpaths over the passes, the volcanoes and the glaciers, a century of logging and what has grown back, and Mount St. Helens quietly rebuilding itself. In conversation with Mary Ann Gwinn, for years the book critic at the Seattle Times; signing afterwards.
$10 general, free for museum members, registration required in advance — the cheapest ticketed evening of the week, and a 6:30 start rather than 7:30, so it ends around eight.
Weather note: Wednesday is 73° and cloudless. A 6:30 start leaves the whole afternoon outside first, and the museum is on the Montlake side of campus if you want to walk the arboretum beforehand.
Ideas & lectures · thursday · free, then $10
Thursday holds two talks back to back: a free one at five that ends at six, and the man who ran the Panama Papers at half past seven
THU SEP 17, 5–6PM FREE  ·  AND 7:30PM, $10–35  ·  UNIVERSITY DISTRICT, THEN FIRST HILL
Five o'clock, free, and it is over at six. Colin Marshall, a philosopher at the University of Washington, launches Just Hear Me Out: How to Change Minds Without Lies, Bullshit, or Moral Compromise, in conversation with his colleague José Jorge Mendoza. It is at the Barnes & Noble in the University District, free with registration, and it is the only University of Washington public lecture before October 8 — the autumn series does not really begin until then. An hour-long evening event that finishes before dinner is a rare shape.
Then, if you want a second one: Gerard Ryle at 7:30, in the Wyncote Northwest Forum at Town Hall, $10–35 sliding. Ryle directs the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists — the outfit that ran the Panama Papers, won the 2017 Pulitzer for it, and invented the model of hundreds of reporters in dozens of countries working one leak together. The Pandora Papers and Cyprus Confidential came after; governments have recovered more than $1.4 billion off the back of the work. This is offshore secrecy told by the person who organised the reporting on it.
The catch: Thursday is 76° and 0%, the best day in the file, and both of these are indoors. The five o'clock one only costs you an hour of it.
