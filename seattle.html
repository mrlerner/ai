This week in Seattle
Water · free · the count finally posted
The Ballard Locks coho backlog came in, and the run is up 66% in three days of counting
SEASON TOTAL 1,612 THROUGH SEP 6  ·  FREE  ·  HIRAM M. CHITTENDEN LOCKS, BALLARD
For six mornings this brief has carried a one-line note that the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife had posted nothing since the 463-fish day on September 3. That note is now retired. The missing days are up: 380 fish on September 4, 13 on the 5th, and 247 on the 6th, taking the coho season total from 972 to 1,612. Chinook are at 11,793 and the sockeye run finished at 31,302. Counting stops at September 6 for now — the daily numbers are marked preliminary and they clearly post in clumps.
What this is worth in person: the fish ladder at the locks has a below-water viewing gallery where the coho stack up in the fourteenth weir, and the whole thing is free and open daily. Peak is still the last two weeks of September, so the run is climbing into it rather than off it — but 380 fish in a day is already a busy window rather than an empty tank.
Weather call: do not do this in the rain on Saturday. Monday through Wednesday is 70°, 72° and 75° with almost no cloud, and the locks plus the Ballard side of the ship canal is a good hour on any of them. Indoor fallback is built into the site — the viewing gallery is underground and dry, so a wet day only ruins the walk in, not the fish.
Nature & sky · free · two clear nights, then it is gone
A thin crescent moon slides past Venus on Sunday and Monday, low in the west — and both nights are forecast clear
SUN SEP 13 & MON SEP 14, ABOUT 7:55PM  ·  FREE  ·  ANY WEST-FACING SHORE
This is the sky event of the week and it is genuinely a Seattle problem: it happens low, so it needs an open western horizon, which in this city means water. Sunset is 7:27pm on Sunday and 7:25pm on Monday. Look about half an hour after, around 7:55, and Venus is the brilliant point a little over five degrees above the west-southwest horizon — roughly three fingers held at arm's length. The moon is a 9% crescent on Sunday, sitting about six degrees to Venus's lower right, and a 16% crescent on Monday, about six degrees to Venus's upper left. It having crossed over is the whole show.
Weather call, and it is the good kind: Sunday night is 9% and Monday night is 1% — the first genuinely clear nights in the file, after a new moon week that spent itself under cloud. There is no indoor fallback for this one; there is only the next clear evening, and Venus is dropping toward the sun from here.
Where to stand: Golden Gardens, Alki, Carkeek, Discovery Park's Sound side — anywhere the Olympics are your skyline. Fifteen minutes of standing gets it. If you are already out, Saturn is rising in the east right after sunset and is up most of the night, and the September epsilon Perseids are still weakly active through the 21st at a handful of meteors an hour, so the same dark half hour pays twice.
Ideas & lectures · monday · correction
Town Hall has double-booked Monday night — two good talks, same building, same 7:30. Pick one.
MON SEP 14, BOTH 7:30PM  ·  $10–35 / $10–129  ·  TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
Correcting this brief's own calendar line: yesterday it read as though Alexis Nikole Nelson and Leah Stokes were one event. They are two, in two different rooms of the same building, starting at the same minute.
Leah Stokes with David Roberts, The Carbon Wave — the Wyncote Northwest Forum, doors 6:30, 7:30 start, about 75 minutes, $10–35 sliding. Stokes is the University of California Santa Barbara political scientist who was inside the drafting of the 2022 climate law; the book runs from the Green New Deal in 2018 through the bill's near-death and surprise passage, to what the second Trump administration has since unwound, braided with her own story of premature twins. Roberts writes the energy newsletter Volts and is the sharpest climate-policy interviewer in the city. Part of Town Hall's environment series.
Alexis Nikole Nelson — the Great Hall, 7:30, $10–129, presented by Seattle Arts & Lectures, the author series that runs a season from autumn to spring. Nelson is the forager with the enormous following, here for her first book, Happy Snacking, Don't Die!, which is a season-by-season field guide with vegan recipes attached; Brandi Fullwood hosts and takes questions after. Ticket page.
Which one: Stokes is the better fit for how you spend your working hours — policy that actually got built, told by someone who built it — and it is the cheaper ticket with a printed run time that gets you out by a quarter to nine. Nelson is the better fit for a night out that is fun rather than instructive. Both are before the 7:55 moon-and-Venus window, so Monday genuinely holds one or the other, not both plus the sky.
Ideas & cities · tuesday · $35
CityNerd walks through an unreleased video on Puget Sound transit, live, with the region's best transit reporter arguing back
TUE SEP 15, 7–8:30PM  ·  $35  ·  THE GREAT HALL, TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
The closest thing this week to work you would do for fun. Ray Delahanty makes the CityNerd videos — the ones that rank American cities on grid density or walk time to a grocery store — and spent two decades as a working transportation planner before that. He is doing a live walkthrough of a video he has not published yet, on the region's recent light rail openings and what station-area development is or is not being built around them, then arguing it out with Ryan Packer, who covers transportation and land use for the local urbanist newspaper The Urbanist and knows where every project is actually stuck.
$35 general admission, 7pm to 8:30pm — a printed end time, and the earliest end of any ticketed talk this week. There is a $65 pre-reception at 5:45; the $35 seat is the same conversation. Tickets go through the venue's page above.
Weather note, since it is indoors on the wrong day: Tuesday is 72° and 0% — the single clearest day in the file. Take the afternoon outside and spend the evening here rather than the reverse.
Nature & ideas · wednesday · $10, free for members
A human and natural history of the Cascades, at the natural history museum, for ten dollars
WED SEP 16, 6:30PM  ·  $10 GENERAL, FREE FOR MEMBERS  ·  BURKE MUSEUM, UNIVERSITY OF WASHINGTON CAMPUS
David B. Williams is the Seattle naturalist who wrote Homewaters, the book about Puget Sound itself. His new one, In the Range of Fire and Ice, does the same job on the Cascades: Indigenous huckleberry grounds and the footpaths that crossed the passes, volcanoes and glaciers, the century of logging and mining and what has come back since, and Mount St. Helens rebuilding itself in real time. He built it out of interviews with anthropologists, ecologists and climate scientists rather than from a desk. He is in conversation with Mary Ann Gwinn, who was the Seattle Times book critic for years and is a real interviewer.
Ten dollars, free if you are a museum member, and it needs registration in advance — the sign-up runs through the museum's ticketing site linked from the event page. Book signing after, with copies from the museum store; the publisher is Mountaineers Books.
Weather call: Wednesday is the warmest day in the file at 75° and 2%. A 6:30 start on the university campus leaves the whole afternoon outdoors first — the locks and the coho are twenty minutes west of there.
Saturday · free and $10 · the 59% answer
Saturday has degraded for the fourth morning in a row. Three ways to spend it under a roof.
SAT SEP 12  ·  RAIN LIKELY AFTER 11AM, 59%, HIGH 68°  ·  COLUMBIA CITY AND FIRST HILL
The free one, all day: the Seattle World Percussion Society's World Rhythm Festival, Friday through Sunday — a hundred-plus drum and dance workshops and performances across African, Middle Eastern, Latin, Indian, Brazilian and North American traditions, pitched from complete beginner upward. Free, donations welcome. Two of its three venues are indoors and that is the point on Saturday: Rainier Arts Center at 3515 South Alaska Street and Southside Commons at 3518 South Edmunds Street, with the outdoor stage at Columbia Park. All of it is blocks from the Columbia City light rail station, so no driving in it. Take a workshop rather than watch one.
The early evening one: the Ray Skjelbred Trio at the Royal Room in the same neighborhood — hot Chicago piano, 1920s and '30s material played straight. Doors 5pm, show 5:30 to 7:30, that end time printed rather than hoped for. $20 in advance online only, $25 at the door.
The later one: Mary Robinette Kowal and Dr Sian Proctor at 7pm at Town Hall — the Hugo-winning novelist behind The Calculating Stars and the geoscientist who flew as pilot on the first all-civilian orbital flight, on what space programs plausibly do next, moderated by K Wilson for the Clarion West writing workshop. $10 in person, $5 to stream it. Ignore the $150 reception ticket; the cheap seat is the same conversation.
Music & food · free · sunday is the weekend
Live Aloha Hawaiian Cultural Festival — free, two stages, and the only decent weekend daylight lands on it
SUN SEP 13, 11AM–6PM  ·  FREE  ·  SEATTLE CENTER (ARMORY, MURAL AMPHITHEATRE, FISHER ROOFTOP)
One of the free Seattle Center Festál cultural festivals, run with the community that puts it on rather than for tourists. Two stages of Hawaiian music and hula run the full seven hours, there is a food hall's worth of Hawaiian cooking in the Armory, a marketplace, hands-on workshops and a children's corner. It is the kind of thing that costs nothing, takes as long as you want to give it, and is over well before dinner.
Weather call: Sunday is 69° and partly sunny at 23%, with the slight rain chance clearing after 5pm — slightly worse than yesterday's read but still far and away the better weekend day. Indoor fallback is built into the site: the Armory Food and Event Hall is covered and the Fisher Rooftop is sheltered, so a shower moves the day indoors rather than ending it.
Then the evening, if you want a second helping: the Columbia City Beatwalk from 4pm on Ferdinand Festival Street — free, family-friendly, live music in the venues and out on the street, themed “Anything Goes,” and the last one of the summer. The first three hours are the ones that fit a nine o'clock bedtime, and they end in time for the moon and Venus.
Music · free to cheap · small rooms
How the free radio sessions actually work, finally pinned down — plus three small rooms next week
SHABAZZ PALACES TODAY  ·  FREE  ·  472 FIRST AVENUE NORTH, SEATTLE CENTER
This brief has said twice that the listener-supported station's in-studio sessions are free and open but that no capacity or start time is published. The station's own viewing-gallery page has the mechanism: you sign up in person at the building on the day, from 90 minutes before the performance; there are 75 seats in the viewing gallery and 70 of them go to sign-ups; and the ticket is forfeited if you have not collected it 15 minutes ahead. Once it starts there is no entry or exit, no phones and no cameras, and it runs about half an hour. Only some sessions are opened to the public, and the ones that are say so on the listing. Today it is Shabazz Palaces — Ishmael Butler's project, the most interesting hip-hop this city has made in twenty years. Next Tuesday, September 15, it is anaiis, the French-Senegalese singer. Start times are still not printed; check the listing the morning of.
Three rooms next week, all cheap and all small. Tuesday, the Royal Room in Columbia City has CASCADIANS: Bioregional Music at 7:30 — music written to and about this particular corner of the continent, which is a strange and local thing to sit in. Wednesday, the same room does Piano Starts Here, its recurring repertory night, on McCoy Tyner and Alice Coltrane — two of the heaviest pianists in the music, played live rather than lectured about, 7:30. Thursday the 17th, Sean Rowe at the Sunset Tavern in Ballard, 7:30 doors — a voice about an octave below where you expect one.
Both Royal Room nights collide with the CityNerd talk and the Burke talk respectively, so they are the alternatives rather than additions. One sold out already: Carsie Blanton at the Tractor Tavern on the 17th.
