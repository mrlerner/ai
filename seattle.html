This Week in Seattle
NATURE & SKY — TONIGHT, AND THE CLOUD FORECAST IMPROVED
Minor geomagnetic storm, Kp 5.33: the northern horizon is worth a look tonight between full dark and ten
WED SEP 23, 8:49–~10PM  ·  FREE  ·  NORTH-FACING SHORELINE, AWAY FROM CITY LIGHT
The forecast held. The Space Weather Prediction Center's three-day forecast, reissued at 5:30 this morning, has not changed: Kp 4.33 from five to eight, 5.33 from eight to eleven (a G1, or minor, storm), 4.67 from eleven to two. The cause is still named as a fast stream of solar wind from a coronal hole. Kp 5 is where the aurora becomes possible low on the northern horizon from Seattle. Expect a pale glow above the northern hills, not curtains overhead. One honest caveat: the highest Kp actually observed in the past 24 hours was 1. So far this is a forecast; the storm hasn't arrived yet.
What changed is the cloud. Yesterday's hourly forecast had cloud cover jumping to 54% at 8pm and staying there. This morning's reads 29% at eight, 36% at nine, then 72% at ten, with no rain all night. So the usable window is about 8:49, when full dark arrives, to ten. That's close to your bedtime, so treat it as a short trip out, not a whole evening.
The moon, recomputed this morning (Jet Propulsion Laboratory ephemeris): 93% lit, 17° up at eight and 24° at nine, in the southeast (azimuth 132° to 145°). The aurora would be due north, so your back is to the moon. It brightens the sky but doesn't sit in your view.
What I'd do: Golden Gardens or Carkeek Park (both in Ballard), or Discovery Park's north bluff. Anywhere with open water to the north and the city glow behind you. Get there by nine, give your eyes ten minutes to adjust, and take a few-second phone exposure facing north even if you see nothing, because at Kp 5 the camera picks up colour your eyes can't. The aurora map at the link updates every half hour: check it before you put your shoes on. If it's blank or the sky has closed over, stay home.
THURSDAY — RAIN FROM FOUR, SO SPEND THE EVENING INDOORS
Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor on the people betting on collapse, with Mayor Katie Wilson moderating
THU SEP 24, 7:30PM (OUT BY ~8:45)  ·  $10–50 SLIDING  ·  TOWN HALL GREAT HALL, FIRST HILL
Still the biggest ticket of the week. Their book is End Times Fascism and the Fight for the Living World. The argument: tech billionaires, religious fundamentalists and ethno-nationalists now share one belief, that a catastrophe is coming and they'll be the ones who survive it. Think bunkers, private islands and Mars rockets. What makes this one different is the moderator: the mayor who runs the city is the one asking what cities should do about that belief. Town Hall says its talks run about 75 minutes, so you're out by 8:45.
Free instead: Christopher W. Smith at the Burke Museum (the natural history museum on the University of Washington campus), 7pm, doors 6:30, free with registration. He talks about the Tsimshian master carver Samuel Elwitt. The event page still wrongly shows "this event is in the past", so register ahead instead of just turning up. Or a film: Life in The Sound, 7pm, Northwest Film Forum on Capitol Hill, $15 or $10. It follows a soundscape ecologist (someone who studies the sounds of a place) for a year; despite the title, it isn't about Puget Sound.
FRIDAY — NEW TO THIS BRIEF; STORMS ARE NOW AT 96%
"Go outside. Bear witness. Make a record. Pass it on." A former Sierra editor on how we forget what nature used to look like
FRI SEP 25, 7:30PM (OUT BY ~8:45)  ·  $10–35 SLIDING  ·  WYNCOTE NORTHWEST FORUM, TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
Jason Dove Mark, longtime editor-in-chief of Sierra magazine, in conversation with Lynda Mapes. His new book is The Earth Said Remember Me. It's about what scientists call shifting baseline syndrome: each generation takes the depleted nature it grew up with as normal. Fewer salmon, fewer birds, thinner forests stop registering as losses. His answer is the four rules in the headline, and his examples are birdwatchers, climbers, fishermen and volunteer scientists who keep records of what they see. Someone who has watched a salmon run over the years is exactly who he's writing for.
The music option, same time, same building: Ganesh Rajagopalan with Marina Albero, 7:30 in the Great Hall, $25–50. He plays South Indian classical violin and she's a Seattle pianist; together they open Town Hall's world-music season with his record Maya. It's a concert, so expect a full-length evening rather than 75 minutes. Free, midday: KEXP's Gathering Space at Seattle Center (the radio station's public hall) is open 10 to 5, all ages, with Hermanos Gutiérrez playing live at noon.
SUNDAY — THE LAST FREE BOAT RIDE UNTIL APRIL
Free wooden-boat rides on Lake Union: the sixth and final date of 2026
SUN SEP 27  ·  FREE  ·  IN-PERSON SIGN-UP FROM 1PM, 1010 VALLEY ST, SOUTH LAKE UNION
Checked again this morning: the Center for Wooden Boats' page still lists September 27 as the last date. Volunteer skippers take people out for 45-minute rides, with boats leaving from 1:15 to 5. Sign-up is first come, in person only, from 1pm, and they say they can't always take everyone in line, so arrive before one. The last Sunday of the month is also Steamship Sunday, so there's a separate line for the 1922 steamship Virginia V, with the last ride at 3.
Weather has improved: 61°, partly sunny, 4% rain (it was 20% yesterday). If it turns, the fallback is the Local Sightings closing night (the Northwest Film Forum's festival of Northwest films), 7pm on Capitol Hill.
Saturday night, the full moon. Moonrise is 6:50pm, before the 7:00 sunset (worked out in Monday's and Tuesday's briefs). The cloud forecast now puts Saturday and Sunday evenings level at 36%, down from Saturday's 42% yesterday, and both nights are dry. Saturday is the actual full moon, so it's still the one to pick. Watch from an east-facing Lake Washington shore (Seward Park, Magnuson, Madrona) and be in place by 6:30.
