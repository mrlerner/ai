This week in Seattle
Someone has already solved the eclipse's one hard problem, and they are setting up telescopes on the right shore at 7:30
THU AUG 27, FROM 7:30PM  ·  FREE, NO REGISTRATION  ·  LAKEWOOD PLAYGROUND, SEWARD PARK, LAKE WASHINGTON SHORE
The deep partial eclipse is Thursday, and Seattle's difficulty with it has always been altitude — the moon rises at about 7:50pm already deep inside the Earth's shadow, and spends the good hour only a few degrees above the horizon, where any treeline or ridge eats it. The Seattle Astronomical Society has picked the answer for you: the amateur-astronomy club runs a free public star party at Seward Park that evening, on the strip of shoreline with open water under an eastern horizon and nothing between you and the moonrise. Volunteers bring their own telescopes and binoculars and hand them around. Maximum is 9:12pm with roughly 96 percent of the disk in shadow — nearly total, most of the face gone copper with one bright rind left on the edge.
New this morning, and the reason this leads: the seven-day file now reaches Thursday night and calls it partly cloudy, low 54°, after a 70° day at an 18 percent rain chance. Partly cloudy is a real chance, not a washout. The bedtime math also works in your favor here — because the moon comes up already eclipsed, the show is at its best early, and it is deeply into shadow well before nine. Nothing to buy, nothing to wear on your eyes; a lunar eclipse is safe with the naked eye. If Thursday clouds over, there is no second night.

The rain call improved overnight, which makes the fiftieth birthday of the first park ever built over a freeway a go
TODAY, 12–3PM  ·  FREE, NO TICKET  ·  FREEWAY PARK, 700 SENECA ST, FIRST HILL
Freeway Park opened on July 4, 1976 as the first park in the world decked over a working interstate — Lawrence Halprin's concrete canyons and waterfalls poured straight above Interstate 5, and the direct ancestor of every lid proposal this city has argued about since. The parkwide celebration runs three hours with architectural tours, live music, temporary artworks from Kate Clark, Julie Reinke Carrier and Amity Preston, a site-specific audio piece by Hannah Simmons and Leah Crosby, and free food, all of it free and none of it ticketed. Put on by the Freeway Park Association with Seattle Parks and Recreation, with future-of-the-lid conversations folded into the afternoon — which is the part that earns the top of the page for someone who writes civic software.
Weather call, revised and much better than yesterday's: the chance of rain is 30 percent and only before 1pm at 75°, so the back two hours of the window look clean. Halprin built the place in overhangs and concrete, so it shelters better than an open lawn anyway. Indoor fallback within four blocks: the Central Library, whose eleven floors are their own architectural argument, and Town Hall Seattle, where the Solstice Symphony Orchestra plays Stravinsky's Firebird and Rachmaninoff's Second Piano Concerto at 7:30pm tonight.

Tuesday came in at eighty degrees — the hottest number in the file, dropped on the one night Lake Union already has plans
TUE AUG 25, FIRST START 7PM  ·  FREE TO WATCH  ·  LAKE UNION — GAS WORKS PARK, OR ON THE WATER
The Duck Dodge is the Tuesday-night sailboat race that has gone around Lake Union every summer for fifty-two years: costumed crews, a fresh theme each week, staggered starts by boat speed from 7pm, and a fleet that is plainly out there for the evening rather than the trophy. Tuesday's forecast climbed to 80° and mostly sunny at a 3 percent rain chance — three degrees warmer than the same day looked yesterday, and the best outdoor evening between now and the Wednesday thunderstorms. The season runs out in September.
Two free ways in: the hill on the north shore at Gas Works looks straight down the course, or put a boat in and watch from inside the spectator fleet — the Northwest Outdoor Center and Agua Verde both rent on the lake, both a short paddle from the start. Racing finishes well before dark. Monday at 75° and zero percent rain is the clean backup, minus the sailboats. Note the collision below: the Ed Park reading is the same evening.
Two days of free music at Seattle Center, and this year they turned the pavilion into a roller rink
TODAY AND SUN AUG 23, DAYTIME  ·  FREE, ALL AGES  ·  SEATTLE CENTER — ARMORY, MURAL AMPHITHEATRE, FISHER PAVILION
Festival Sundiata presents Black Arts Fest, one of the free cultural festivals Seattle Center runs all summer under the Festál banner, spread this weekend across the Armory food hall, the outdoor Mural Amphitheatre and Fisher Pavilion. The music runs from Morris Day and The Time — the Minneapolis funk band out of the same early-eighties scene as Prince — through Zimbabwean-American rapper Draze, the Pacific Northwest Community Gospel Choir, the Double Dutch Divas and a long local bill. New this year is Skate Sundiata, which converts Fisher Pavilion into a roller rink with a live DJ, open to skaters at any level.
The best argument for this one is that it costs nothing and happens in daylight, so bedtime never enters into it, and the food is the point as much as the stage — local vendors through the Armory hall. Weather: today's showers should be done by 1pm, and Sunday is 74° and partly sunny at 6 percent, the safer of the two days. Everything in the Armory and Fisher Pavilion is under a roof regardless, which makes this the rare outdoor pick that carries its own indoor fallback.

A quiet record turns twenty and gets played end to end in a room small enough to hear it
SUN AUG 23, 7:30PM  ·  $27  ·  21+  ·  TRACTOR TAVERN, BALLARD AVENUE
Horse Feathers — the Portland string band built on fingerpicked guitar under cello and fiddle, quiet enough that a loud room ruins it — marks the twentieth anniversary of “Words Are Dead” by playing the album whole, with The Hackles opening, presented by the Bellevue community radio station KBCS. The Tractor holds a few hundred people under wagon wheels on Ballard Avenue and is exactly the right size for a band that plays this softly.
The 7:30 Sunday start is the friendliest slot the room offers all week — every other Tractor show in the next seven days is an 8pm or 8:30pm door. Indoors, so the forecast does not decide it. If Sunday is better spent outside, the whole afternoon before it is free: Black Arts Fest at Seattle Center, then twelve minutes north to Ballard.

The night the thunderstorms arrive is also the night the natural-history talk is indoors and starts at ten dollars
WED AUG 26, DOORS 6:30PM, TALK 7:30PM  ·  $10–$35 SLIDING SCALE  ·  TOWN HALL SEATTLE, FIRST HILL
Artist and naturalist Emily Poole launches “Cascade of Life: An Artist's Seasonal Guide to the Wild Northwest,” a field guide arranged by season rather than by species — shoreline to alpine to shrub-steppe, illustration beside essay. She is joined by Kate Rogers, editor-in-chief of Mountaineers Books, the nonprofit Seattle publisher, and Katie Campbell, digital editor and reporter at the public radio station KUOW. The evening doubles as a preview of the publisher's entire fall art-and-natural-history list, which is the part worth the ticket even if the one book is not for you.
Timing is the argument this week: Wednesday is the one day carrying thunderstorms, 72° with a 28 percent chance between 11am and 5pm and rain behind it, so the outdoor options that day are poor anyway. Town Hall runs these at roughly seventy-five minutes, which puts you out around 8:45. The sliding scale starts at ten dollars and is not means-tested.

A memoir written on a floppy disk in 1998, lost for a quarter century, and read aloud for free in Lake Forest Park
TUE AUG 25, 7PM  ·  FREE, RSVP REQUESTED  ·  THIRD PLACE BOOKS, 17171 BOTHELL WAY NE, LAKE FOREST PARK
Ed Park — the novelist whose “Same Bed Different Dreams” was a Pulitzer finalist — presents “Three Tenses,” which he wrote on a floppy disk in 1998, lost, and rediscovered decades later: a transmission from the nineties published essentially as it was found. He is in conversation with the Seattle novelist Thomas Kohnstamm. Third Place Books is the big bookstore in the Lake Forest Park shopping center north of the lake, and its author events are free with an online RSVP.
The honest problem: this is the same Tuesday evening as the Duck Dodge on the warmest, sunniest day of the week, and a 7pm reading indoors is the wrong use of 80°. Take this one if Tuesday clouds up or if the lake is not the mood — it is free either way, indoors either way, and out by eight.

Four chances at a hand-painted French science-fiction cartoon, and two of them start before five
SUN AUG 23, 5PM  ·  MON AUG 24, 7:30PM  ·  TUE & WED, 4:30PM  ·  THE BEACON, 4405 RAINIER AVE S, COLUMBIA CITY
“Gandahar,” René Laloux's 1988 animated feature — the last of the three he made, after “Fantastic Planet” — runs four nights at the Beacon, the small repertory theater above the Columbia City neighborhood's main street that programs almost nothing you can see anywhere else. It closes out a run that ends Wednesday, and it is the sort of thing that surfaces once every few years and then disappears back into unavailability.
The 4:30pm Tuesday and Wednesday shows are the useful ones — a feature that ends before dinner, and on Wednesday it pairs with the storms outside. The Beacon sells single tickets and runs a membership; check the calendar page for the current show's price. Sunday's 5pm is the other early option, but Sunday is also the Horse Feathers night in Ballard, which is the opposite corner of the city.
