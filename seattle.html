NATURE & SKY — THE FIRST LIVE NATURAL EVENT IN A WEEK
Two thousand coho went up the ladder on Monday. The run is only a third in.
ANY EVENING THIS WEEK  ·  FREE  ·  BALLARD LOCKS FISH LADDER, 7AM–8:45PM DAILY
Here are the actual numbers, pulled this morning. The state wildlife department and the Muckleshoot Indian Tribe count every salmon that climbs the ladder at the Hiram M. Chittenden Locks — the Ballard Locks — on its way from Puget Sound into Lake Washington. The coho tally so far this year: 8,739 fish through Monday Sep 15. The daily column is where the story is. Sep 10: 1,774. Sep 13: 1,612. Sep 14: 2,000 — the biggest single day of the run. Compare that with the first week of September, which was running 13 to 300 fish a day.
Why that means go now. A Lake Washington coho year averages around 25,000 fish. At 8,739 the run is roughly a third counted, which puts the thick of it across the next two or three weeks — and the density in that ladder is what makes it worth standing in front of. The Chinook are the other half of the picture and they are going the other way: 13,874 counted, against an average year of about 15,000, and the daily count has fallen to 96, then 7. So this week is the handover — the last of the king salmon and the front of the silvers in the same twenty-one windows.
What it actually is: an underground room with a wall of thick glass set into the side of the fish ladder, so you are looking sideways into the water at fish holding and then shooting up through the weir. It takes ten minutes or two hours, depending on you. Free, no ticket, no booking. Fish ladder viewing room open 7am to 8:45pm; the grounds 7am to 9pm. 3015 Northwest 54th Street, Ballard — the Corps' own hours page, which is where those times come from, is here. Sunny and 73° today, 71° tomorrow, so the botanical garden on the north side is a pleasant half hour on the way in. Rain answer: the viewing room is indoors and underground — weather is irrelevant to the fish part, which is the point of putting it in a week when everything else needs sun.
Tides and aurora, seventh morning running: nothing. No low below zero anywhere in the eight-day file, every flag null, and today's lowest low is 0.4 feet at half past three in the morning. The planetary K-index peaks at 4.7 this afternoon — daylight here — and the script's call is no aurora at this latitude. The fish are the natural event this week.

NEW ON THE CALENDAR — AND IT IS THE BIG ONE
The Jaipur Literature Festival is in Seattle for the weekend
SAT SEP 19, 10AM–5:45PM & SUN SEP 20, 10AM–2:45PM  ·  $45–80  ·  TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
The Jaipur Literature Festival is the enormous annual books-and-ideas festival held in Rajasthan, in northern India — free, open-air, several hundred thousand people. It travels a handful of international editions a year, and this is the third time it has come to Seattle. Fifteen conversations across two days in the two Town Hall rooms, back to back on the hour. It was not on this brief's radar until the calendar pull this morning, and for a week thin on lectures it is far and away the most ideas per dollar available.
The Saturday sessions worth the ticket: Caroline Elkins at 3pm — the Harvard historian whose Imperial Reckoning won the Pulitzer Prize for the detention camps of late British Kenya, here on Legacy of Violence. Scott Anderson at 4pm on the Iranian revolution. Yann Martel at 5pm, who wrote Life of Pi, with his new novel Son of Nobody, in conversation with the Seattle writer Kristen Millares Young. Earlier in the day: Pria Anand at 1pm, a neurologist on what strange brains tell us about being a person, and a 12pm panel with Kanwal Rekhi and Vinita Gupta — he took Excelan public in 1987, the first Indian-American-founded venture-backed company to list on the Nasdaq; she founded Digital Link and in 1994 became the first Indian-American woman to take her own company public.
Sunday is shorter and, for you, possibly better. Noon: "Co-intelligence and Control: The AI Takeover" with Soenke Ziesche. 1pm: Sam Dalrymple on Shattered Lands, which argues the modern map of Asia was made by five partitions, not one — the sort of argument that rearranges a mental map permanently. 2pm: the filmmaker Shekhar Kapur, who made Elizabeth and Bandit Queen. Music at 10. Done by a quarter to three, which leaves the whole sunny afternoon.
How to get in: $45 to $80 — the range covers a full festival pass and single-day passes for Saturday or Sunday separately, all three sold from the event page. In person, no livestream. Town Hall Seattle, 1119 Eighth Avenue on First Hill; the sessions split between the Great Hall upstairs and the Wyncote Northwest Forum downstairs, and the programme tells you which. Doors-open time is not published for individual sessions, so allow the usual quarter hour.
The honest scheduling note: this collides with the two free outdoor things below — the Indigenous festival on the waterfront runs all day Saturday, and Sunday afternoon has the jazz. A single-day Sunday pass gets you out at 2:45, which is the combination that works: festival in the morning, sunshine after.

FREE — AND THE SAME FISH, ON THE OTHER SIDE OF THE STORY
Canoes land at noon on the downtown waterfront
SAT SEP 19, 10AM–8PM  ·  FREE, ALL AGES  ·  PIERS 58 & 62, CENTRAL WATERFRONT
Salmon Homecoming is thirty-four years old and is the tribal counterpart to the fish counts above: the salmon return, and the Coast Salish and other Northwest tribes mark it with a gathering. This year it spreads across two piers instead of one. The hinge of the day is the canoe landing ceremony at noon on Pier 62 — canoe families paddle in off Elliott Bay and ask permission to come ashore, which is the protocol, and it is a genuinely moving thing to watch from a few feet away rather than read about.
The rest of the day, by the clock: Native vendor marketplace 10am–7pm across both piers; a weaving workshop 10am–3pm on Pier 62; food from 11am to 6pm — salmon plates and Indian tacos, which is the good version of festival food; and cultural sharing on Pier 58 from 1pm to 8pm, songs and dances from Black River, Swaq'ʷsəb and other tribal groups. Conservation is the other half of its purpose, so the booths are salmon-habitat organisations rather than a craft fair.
The weather call: Saturday is 70° and mostly sunny at 0% rain — open piers with no cover, so this is exactly what a dry Saturday is for. Free, no ticket, though the organisers ask for a response so they can plan. Piers 58 and 62 are the rebuilt waterfront park at the foot of Union Street, walkable from the ferry terminal and the light rail at University Street. Indoor fallback if it turns: the film festival in Ballard below is running all Saturday, 11am onward.

FILM — CLOSES SATURDAY
A week of Nordic cinema in Ballard, $14 a film, three nights left
THU–SAT SEP 17–19  ·  $14 PER SCREENING ($12 MEMBERS)  ·  NATIONAL NORDIC MUSEUM, BALLARD
The SEA-Nordic Film Festival runs in the Osberg Great Hall at the National Nordic Museum in Ballard and finishes Saturday. This year's programme traces queer representation across Nordic cinema from the silent era to now — a proper curated arc rather than a grab-bag, with short pre-film talks from academics and artists built into the schedule.
What is left, with the times: tonight, the Swedish short Chavo at 6:00 then Iceland's Heartstone at 6:15, out at 8:25. Friday, a discussion at 6:00 and And Then We Danced at 6:30, the Georgian-Swedish film about a dancer in the Tbilisi national ensemble, out at 8:30. Saturday is the full day: Carl Theodor Dreyer's Michael from 1924 at 11am, Girls Lost at 2pm, Tom of Finland at 4:30 after a 4pm talk, and Norway's Dreams at 7:30, which runs to 9:30.
The picks for an early evening: tonight's double is the cleanest — starts at six, you are out by 8:25. Saturday's 11am silent Dreyer and 2pm Girls Lost are both daytime and both finish with the afternoon intact. The 7:30 Saturday film is the one that runs past your bedtime; it ends at 9:30.
How to get in: $14 a screening, $12 if you are a member — a Saturday pass is $52 ($44 members) and a full festival pass $100 ($90), neither of which is worth it at this stage. 2655 Northwest Market Street, ten minutes' walk from the locks, which makes an obvious pairing: fish first, film after.

MUSIC — A JAZZ SHOW THAT ENDS AT SEVEN
The Tula's reunion: four hours of jazz on a Sunday afternoon
SUN SEP 20, 3–7PM  ·  $27.50 ADVANCE / $35 DOOR  ·  THE GEORGETOWN STABLES
Tula's was the jazz club at 2214 Second Avenue in Belltown — the room where the Seattle scene actually played, nightly, for about twenty-six years until it closed in 2019. The players have got back together for an afternoon every year since; this is the fourth of them, put on by Seattle Jazz Offering, and it is pegged specifically to the club's 1999–2014 stretch.
The band is the reason. Randy Halberstadt on keys, who taught half the pianists in this city at Cornish; Phil Sparks on bass and D'Vonne Lewis on drums, which is about as good a Seattle rhythm section as exists; Hans Teuber on saxophone; Reggie Goings singing. The guest is Cipher Goings, a tap dancer just off a Riverdance tour — tap with a live rhythm section is a percussion duet, not a novelty.
Why this one and not a Sunday night show: it is three in the afternoon to seven in the evening. You get a full four hours of music and you are home well before nine, which almost nothing in jazz allows. All ages, family-friendly, indoor and outdoor space, food available to buy.
How to get in: $27.50 online through tomorrow, $35 at the door on the day — so buying ahead saves $7.50 each. The Georgetown Stables, 980 South Nebraska Street, in Georgetown south of downtown; parking is easy down there in a way it is not in Ballard. Sunday is 69° and sunny, and there is an outdoor side to the venue, so the forecast is on your side either way.

LAST CALL — BOTH OF THESE GO TONIGHT AND TOMORROW
Panama Papers tonight, how to fight well tomorrow
THU & FRI, 7:30PM  ·  $10–35 SLIDING, EACH  ·  TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
Tonight, and this is the last morning it can be flagged: Gerard Ryle, who runs the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists, on what the Panama Papers actually changed in the decade since — governments have recovered at least $1.4 billion and a run of transparency laws moved, which is an unusually large natural experiment in whether disclosure does anything. He is opposite Jas Randhawa, who ran financial-crime compliance at Stripe and Airwallex, so the second chair has to live inside those rules rather than report on them. Wyncote Northwest Forum, doors 6:30, talk 7:30, about 75 minutes, out by 8:45. Sliding scale from $10, in person only.
Free, and first: Colin Marshall, the University of Washington philosopher, launches Just Hear Me Out: How to Change Minds Without Lies, Bullshit, or Moral Compromise — 5–6pm, free with registration, at the Barnes & Noble at 4324 University Way Northeast. Two talks, one Thursday, ten dollars all in. This is still the only University of Washington public lecture before Oct 8 — a fourth straight morning unchanged, with the six withdrawn signature-series invitations still posted on the lectures page.
Tomorrow, the Great Hall: Priya Parker, who wrote The Art of Gathering, on The Art of Fighting: The Transformative Power of Conflict. Her argument is that groups which never fight are not healthy, only conflict-avoidant, and that there is a craft to disagreeing that leaves the room stronger. She is a conflict facilitator by trade — this is practitioner material, not a think piece. With Aaron Hurst, who founded the Taproot Foundation. 7:30pm, doors 6:30, $10–35 sliding plus an optional book, in person only.
