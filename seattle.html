THE LAST 71° WEEKEND — AND BOTH OF SUNDAY’S ARE FREE
A free festival at Seattle Center in the late morning, a floating concert on Lake Union in the afternoon
SUN SEP 20  ·  11AM–6PM AND 3–6PM  ·  BOTH FREE  ·  SEATTLE CENTER, THEN LAKE UNION
The forecast is the argument for doing both. Sunday is 71°, mostly sunny, zero percent rain, north wind two to six. So is Monday, and then it is over: Tuesday 68°, Wednesday 67° with rain in the evening, and the week ends at 63°. This is the last warm dry Sunday the model is currently offering.
Late morning — Sea Mar Fiestas Patrias, 11am to 6pm, free, all ages. This is one of the Festál series, the year-round run of free cultural festivals Seattle Center hosts on its campus; this one marks the Mexican and Central American independence days. It runs across the Armory food hall, Fisher Pavilion and the Fisher rooftop, so there is an indoor half if the sun does something unexpected. Sunday is the second and quieter of its two days — Saturday runs to nine at night and takes the bigger crowd. One practical note from the venue: the International Fountain is closed for maintenance from Sunday through Friday the 25th, so that is not the thing to promise anyone.
Afternoon — the paddle-out concert on Lake Union, 3 to 6pm, free, and you get there by boat. Lake Union Concerts anchor a stage on a barge off the west shore of Gas Works Park and everyone floats around it. This is the eighth and final one of the 2026 season — nothing like it comes back before next summer. Seafair are partnering on this one and bringing the Trek Ferry, so it is a bigger rig than the usual: proper speakers and a screen. Three local disc jockeys, Fame Sounds, N8VBOY and Average Citizens.
Two things about it that keep it on your list. It is 3 to 6pm — off the water and dry well before dinner, home long before nine. And the organisers’ own terms require a Coast Guard-approved life jacket and ban alcohol and intoxicants outright, on the sensible grounds that this is a lake. Registration is free; the button reads “tickets from $0.00” and reserving a spot is the whole transaction.
Getting on the water: the Gas Works shoreline put-in is closed for construction, so the organisers point you at the Sunnyside Boat Launch, the Fremont Canal, or the Lake Washington Rowing Club dock — a short flat crossing from any of them, and a north wind of six makes it flatter. To rent rather than haul: the Northwest Outdoor Center on west Lake Union, or Agua Verde on Portage Bay. If the weather turns — it should not, but — the festival is the fallback, because half of it is under a roof.

NATURE & SKY — THE ONE NIGHT THE FULL MOON RISES IN DAYLIGHT
The Harvest Moon comes up over the Cascades at 6:50 on Saturday the 26th — ten minutes before the sun goes down
THU SEP 24 – SUN SEP 27  ·  MOONRISE 6:15, 6:30, 6:50, 7:05PM  ·  FREE  ·  ANY EAST-FACING SHORE
The equinox is Tuesday at 5:06 in the afternoon — computed this morning, not looked up: the instant the sun’s apparent position crosses the autumn point. The full moon nearest that instant is the one that gets called the Harvest Moon, and this year it falls four days later, on Saturday September 26, at 99.8% lit. The next full moon, October 26, is a month off the mark. So the 26th is it.
Here is the part that is actually worth planning around, and it is a real effect rather than folklore. The moon normally comes up about fifty minutes later each night. Around the September full moon its path sits at a shallow angle to the horizon here, and the gap collapses. Computed for Seattle this morning: Thursday 6:15pm, Friday 6:30, Saturday 6:50, Sunday 7:05, Monday 7:30. That is fifteen to twenty-five minutes a night instead of fifty — four consecutive evenings with a nearly full moon coming up at roughly dinner time, which is the whole reason the harvest got its name.
Saturday the 26th is the pick of the four. Sunset that evening is 7:00:30 and moonrise is 6:50 — the full moon clears the horizon ten minutes before the sun sets, so for a few minutes you have both of them in the sky at once, the moon coming up in real daylight rather than into the dark. It rises at an azimuth of 81 degrees, a shade north of due east, which puts it up out of the Cascade foothills.
Where to stand: you want an unobstructed eastern horizon over water, which in this city means the Lake Washington shore — Seward Park, Magnuson Park, Madrona Beach, or the Mount Baker shoreline. Get there by 6:30. The moon is big and orange for about the first fifteen minutes and ordinary after that; the whole event is over by a quarter past seven, which for once is a sky event that fits entirely inside your evening. Free, obviously.
Weather call — and this is the caveat. Saturday the 26th is past the seven-day forecast, so there is no number for it yet. What there is: the week trends wetter and cooler from Wednesday, so treat Thursday 6:15 and Friday 6:30 as the backup nights, both of which are nearly as good and both of which will be inside the forecast by tomorrow’s brief. This brief will carry the Saturday number as soon as it exists. No event page and therefore no image on this card.
IDEAS — WITH THE MAYOR OF SEATTLE ON STAGE
Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor on the people betting on collapse — moderated by Mayor Katie Wilson
THU SEP 24, 7:30PM  ·  $10–50 SLIDING  ·  TOWN HALL GREAT HALL, 1119 EIGHTH AVE, FIRST HILL
This was not on the calendar yesterday morning and it is the best ideas booking of the month. Klein and Taylor are here on their new book End Times Fascism and the Fight for the Living World, whose argument is a specific one rather than a general lament: that religious fundamentalists, Silicon Valley technologists and ethno-nationalists have converged on the same belief — that a cleansing catastrophe is coming — and have stopped trying to prevent it. The book is about the people building the bunkers, buying the islands and funding the rockets, and about where that coalition is weak.
The reason to go rather than read it: Mayor Katie Wilson is moderating. That is a sitting city executive, on stage, taking a book about elite withdrawal from shared institutions and having to answer for the city she actually runs. If you want to hear how national argument survives contact with municipal reality — permits, budgets, a housing pipeline — this is the hour where it gets tested. That is the version of this conversation that is any use to somebody building civic software.
How to get in: $10 to $50 on a sliding scale, with an optional book add-on at checkout — the ten-dollar tier is a real ticket, not a standing-room consolation. The Great Hall is the big upstairs room, so there is capacity, but a mayor-moderated Klein event will not sit around unsold; book it today. 7:30 start, and a Town Hall author conversation plus questions is reliably about ninety minutes, which puts you out around nine.
Thursday is crowded, so here is the honest conflict. At 7pm, free, the Burke Museum has the Vancouver scholar Christopher W. Smith on the Tsimshian master carver Samuel Elwitt, doors 6:30 — the only free talk on the week’s calendar. And the film festival two cards down screens Life in The Sound at 7, a year with a soundscape ecologist studying how sound acts on people. All three at once. Weather: Thursday is the 20% evening — rain after five — so an indoor Thursday is what the forecast wanted anyway.

NATURE LEARNING — ON THE EVENING OF THE EQUINOX
Old-growth Cascadia read one canopy layer at a time, through the birds that live in each
TUE SEP 22, 7:30PM  ·  $10–35 SLIDING  ·  WYNCOTE NORTHWEST FORUM, TOWN HALL, 1119 EIGHTH AVE
Marina Richie’s Feathered Forest: Aloft with Birds in Ancient Trees takes the structure of an old-growth forest — floor, understory, mid-canopy, crown — and works up through it, using the bird that specialises in each layer to explain what that layer is for. It is a book about why a two-hundred-year-old tree is a different object from a young one, told without requiring you to already care about birds. She is in conversation with Maria Mudd Ruth, who wrote the book on the marbled murrelet, the seabird that flies inland to nest in old-growth canopy and whose discovery in 1974 rewrote a lot of Northwest forest policy.
The timing is the argument for this one. The tide and aurora feeds have now been quiet for nine consecutive mornings, so there is no natural event this week to go and stand in — and this is the evening of the equinox itself, 5:06pm Tuesday. A forest book two hours after the season formally turns is the closest thing on the calendar to marking it.
Practicalities: $10 to $35 sliding, the downstairs Wyncote Northwest Forum — the small room, maybe two hundred seats, which is the right size for this. 7:30 start, author conversations there run about seventy-five minutes, so out around a quarter to nine. Tuesday is 68° and dry, the last clean day of the run, so this costs you a good evening — the trade is that Wednesday onwards the evenings are the wet part of the day anyway.
Town Hall has double-booked its two rooms at 7:30 that night: upstairs in the Great Hall is the novelist Emily St. John Mandel, $10–128, with a new book set in a post-collapse Los Angeles. Same start, same building, same $10 floor — a genuine either/or.
