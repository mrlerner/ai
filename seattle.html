NEW ON THE CALENDAR — AND THE BEST HOUR OF THE WEEK
The man who ran the Panama Papers, on what actually changed
THU SEP 17, 7:30PM (DOORS 6:30, OUT ~8:45)  ·  $10–35 SLIDING  ·  TOWN HALL, FIRST HILL
This was not on the calendar yesterday. Gerard Ryle is the executive director of the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists — the outfit that took a leaked hard drive of offshore company records and turned it into the Panama Papers, which won the 2017 Pulitzer Prize for explanatory journalism and brought down a prime minister or two. The talk is titled How the Panama Papers Changed the World, and the honest framing is the interesting part: a decade on, what did the biggest document leak in journalism actually move?
The numbers he has to work with: governments have clawed back at least $1.4 billion, a run of transparency laws changed, and the business of doing journalism across borders got rebuilt around the model his team invented. He is in conversation with Jas Randhawa, who ran financial-crime and compliance work at Stripe, Airwallex and PwC and now runs a risk consultancy — so the other chair is someone who has to live inside those rules, not just report on them. That is a better pairing than the usual author-and-admirer setup.
Why it is worth your evening: this is a talk about what happens after the investigation — whether disclosure regimes do anything, and what it takes to make a public record change behaviour. If you spend your days building things that turn civic information into something people can act on, that is your subject, told by someone with an unusually large natural experiment to point at.
How to get in: sliding scale from $10 to $35, you pick. In-person only, no livestream. Wyncote Northwest Forum — the smaller downstairs room at Town Hall, 1119 Eighth Avenue, entrance off Seneca Street, First Hill. Doors 6:30, talk 7:30, about 75 minutes, which puts you on the street by a quarter to nine.
Make it a double bill: Colin Marshall, the University of Washington philosopher, launches Just Hear Me Out the same afternoon, 5–6pm, free with registration, at the Barnes & Noble at 4324 University Way Northeast in the U District — his argument is that persuasion which does not cheat is both possible and more effective. Two talks, one Thursday, $10 total. That remains the only University of Washington public lecture before Oct 8: the lectures office has withdrawn invitations to six speakers in its signature series pending a review of how they were chosen, and the autumn is thin as a result.

OPENS TOMORROW — AND ALL FOUR DAYS ARE DRY
A hundred and twenty boats in the water, and you walk aboard them
THU–SUN SEP 17–20  ·  FROM $24  ·  SOUTH LAKE UNION PIERS, 901 FAIRVIEW AVE N
The distinction that matters: this is not a convention-centre boat show with hulls up on trailers under fluorescent light. The boats are in the lake and you climb on them — more than 120 power and sailing craft tied along the South Lake Union piers, most of them open to walk through. It is the closest thing Seattle has to an afternoon of poking around other people's boats without having to know anyone who owns one.
Hours: Thursday and Friday 11am–6pm, the weekend opening earlier at 10am, and Sunday closing at 5. Free seminars run through the days and there is live music on the docks.
How to get in: $24 at the gate for a single day, $40 for a multi-day, teenagers 13–17 $7, under-12s free, $15 on a military identification. Buying online is discounted and that window closes when the show does on Sunday.
The weather call: all four days are now dry and 70–73°, so pick on crowds rather than sky. Thursday or Friday gets you emptier docks — a weekday morning on a pier you can actually move along is a different experience from Saturday. If you want the weekend anyway, take Sunday: the show shuts at 5 and the floating concert half a mile up the same lake starts at 3, which makes one long day on the water out of the two. Fallback: it is a dock, so there is no indoor version — but with seven dry days in the file this is the least weather-exposed plan in the brief.

ALSO NEW — FRIDAY'S TALK
Priya Parker on fighting well — the sequel to the gathering book
FRI SEP 18, 7:30PM (DOORS 6:30, OUT ~8:45)  ·  $10–35  ·  TOWN HALL GREAT HALL
Priya Parker wrote The Art of Gathering, the book that talked a lot of people out of running meetings and dinners on autopilot. The new one is The Art of Fighting: The Transformative Power of Conflict, and it is the harder argument: that the groups which last are not the ones that avoid disagreement but the ones that can have it on purpose. She has spent twenty years facilitating conflict for organisations and communities, which is a real trade rather than a speaking-circuit pose.
Who is in the other chair: Aaron Hurst, who founded the Taproot Foundation and the workplace-purpose company Imperative, and now runs something called the US Chamber of Connection. So the conversation is pitched at people who build institutions rather than at readers looking for dinner-party advice.
How to get in: $10–35, with an optional book add-on. In-person only — no livestream on this one. The Great Hall, the big upstairs room at Town Hall, 1119 Eighth Avenue, First Hill. Doors 6:30, talk 7:30, about 75 minutes, out around a quarter to nine.
If Friday is meant to be outdoors instead: the free band-then-film night in the U District runs again — Hockey Teeth, a power-pop quartet, at 6pm in the Square at U District Station, NE 43rd and Brooklyn Avenue NE, with Grease after at 7:45. The 6pm set is the part that fits; the film runs past half nine. And Chelsea Wolfe plays the radio station's studio at 11am, free and open to the public, for half an hour — a weekday-morning hole rather than an evening, but she normally plays rooms a hundred times that size.

NOW PINNED DOWN — FREE, BOTH WEEKEND DAYS
Latin American independence weekend at Seattle Center — mariachi, cumbia and free
SAT SEP 19 12–9PM, SUN SEP 20 11AM–6PM  ·  FREE  ·  SEATTLE CENTER, UPTOWN
Last week's brief flagged that something from the Seattle Center festival series landed this weekend but could not say which one. It is Sea Mar Fiestas Patrias — the celebration of the Latin American independence days, most of which fall in September. It is one of the 25 free cultural festivals the campus runs through the year, and this is one of the biggest.
The music is the reason to go: the main stage in Fisher Pavilion runs from Andean groups through to Mariachi Wenatchee, and closes out with Sonora Dinamita, a cumbia band with a fifty-year back catalogue. Around it: traditional folk dance, cooking demonstrations, an art exhibition, craft and food vendors, Zumba classes, a soccer clinic run by the Sounders, and a health fair with free screenings. This year's theme translates as "Healthy Communities, Vibrant Cultures."
How to get in: free, no ticket, all ages. Armory Food & Event Hall, Fisher Pavilion and the Fisher rooftop, Seattle Center, Uptown. Saturday runs noon to 9, Sunday 11 to 6. A parade steps off in South Park at 10am Saturday if you want the start of it.
The weather call: 70° and mostly sunny both days, and much of this sits under a roof anyway — the Armory and Fisher Pavilion are indoor buildings — so it is the one weekend pick that does not care what the sky does. Which day: Saturday is already crowded with the artists' takeover downtown and the symphony's opening night, so Sunday late morning is the cleaner slot, and it leaves the afternoon free for the lake.

FOUR DAYS OUT — STILL FREE, STILL DRY
Take the kayak to a concert on Sunday afternoon
SUN SEP 20, 3–6PM  ·  FREE  ·  LAKE UNION, OFF GAS WORKS PARK
Still the single best use of a boat this week. The music plays from a vessel anchored off the west side of Gas Works Park and the audience is whatever paddles up — kayaks, boards, canoes. Sunday is the last one of the year, the eighth and final show of a series that started as a few hundred paddleboarders in the summer of 2025, and it does not come back until next summer.
What is different about the finale: the organisers have brought in Seafair — the outfit behind the August hydroplane weekend — which is floating a retired Alaskan ferry to serve as the stage, so there is a real stage, real speakers and a screen instead of the small decorated boat the summer shows used. Three local disc jockeys: Fame Sounds, N8VBOY, Average Citizens. Witch costumes are the theme and entirely optional.
How to get in: free — the ticket page still lists $0.00, with roughly 700 claimed. Bring your own boat and a Coast Guard-approved life jacket, which the organisers require you to wear. Their terms also specify no alcohol on the water.
Where to put in: not Gas Works — construction has closed the shore access. They point people at the Sunnyside Avenue North launch, the Fremont canal, or the Lake Washington Rowing Club dock. The Northwest Outdoor Center on Westlake rents boats if you would rather not car-top one.
The weather call: Sunday is 70°, mostly sunny, no rain, and 3–6pm has you off the water long before dark. One correction to yesterday's advice: this is no longer the last dry afternoon of the run — Monday and Tuesday now come in dry too — so it is worth going for its own sake rather than out of urgency about the sky. Fallback: a floating concert is weather-dependent in a way an indoor one is not; check the organiser's page Sunday morning.

NEW OVERNIGHT — FREE, AND AN AFTERNOON FOR ONCE
Stand in the radio studio while Sweeping Promises play — Monday, 3pm, nothing
MON SEP 21, 3–3:30PM  ·  FREE  ·  KEXP STUDIO, NORTHWEST ROOMS, SEATTLE CENTER
The station's in-studio sessions are open to the public and free, with a few dozen people standing close enough to the band to hear the amps rather than the mix. This one appeared on the calendar overnight and is the best-timed of the three now posted: 3pm on a Monday rather than the usual 11am, which is a far easier half hour to take.
Sweeping Promises are a two-piece who record live into a single microphone in odd rooms — a disused chemistry lab for the first record — and the result is taut, bright post-punk that sounds like it was made in one take, because more or less it was. Thirty minutes, broadcast live, video up later.
How to get in: free, through the station's in-studio sign-up linked from the event page — the capacity is small, so it is worth doing in advance rather than turning up. Northwest Rooms at Seattle Center, Uptown, the same campus as the festival two days earlier.
Two more now posted: Iguana Death Cult, a Rotterdam band who play something between krautrock and post-punk, Wed Sep 23, 11–11:30am; and Hermanos Gutiérrez, the Swiss-Ecuadorian guitar duo whose desert instrumentals normally fill theatres, Fri Sep 25, 12–12:30pm — that last one is the one to set a reminder for.

NATURE & SKY — THE FASTEST-CHANGING FORTNIGHT OF THE YEAR
You are losing three and a half minutes of daylight a day right now
EQUINOX TUE SEP 22, 5:06PM  ·  TALK SAME NIGHT 7:30PM, $10–35
The number worth carrying around this week. Seattle has 12 hours 34 minutes of daylight today. By next Wednesday it is 12 hours 10 minutes, and by Saturday Sep 26 it is 11 hours 59 minutes. That is a loss of about 3 minutes 25 seconds every single day — the steepest rate of the whole year, because the sun's path is crossing the horizon at its sharpest angle. Nowhere else on the calendar does the evening shorten this fast.
Two dates that fall out of that: the autumn equinox lands Tuesday Sep 22 at 5:06pm our time, computed from the solar ephemeris. But the folk version is off — Tuesday still has 12 hours 13 minutes of daylight (sunrise 6:55, sunset 7:08), because the sun is a disc and the atmosphere bends its light over the horizon. The genuinely twelve-hour day comes later: Saturday Sep 26 is the first day of 2026 with under twelve hours of daylight here, at 11 hours 59 minutes and 57 seconds. Friday the 25th still has 12 hours 3 minutes. That Saturday is also the harvest moon, 99.7% lit — the full moon nearest the equinox.
Tides and aurora, sixth morning running: nothing. No low below zero anywhere in the eight-day file, every flag null, and the lows that do dip all land between 2:46 and 4:21 in the morning. The planetary K-index peaks at 4.7 this afternoon, which is daylight here, and the call is no aurora at this latitude. Nothing to pounce on.
So the nature learning is the talk. Marina Richie on Feathered Forest: Aloft with Birds in Ancient Trees — the old-growth forests of this region read floor to canopy, with the resident birds as the guide to each layer. She wrote the belted kingfisher book. In conversation with Maria Mudd Ruth, who wrote Rare Bird about the marbled murrelet, the seabird that nests in these same trees and eluded anyone trying to find its nest until 1974. Wyncote Northwest Forum, 1119 Eighth Avenue. Doors 6:30, talk 7:30, about 75 minutes, out by a quarter to nine. Sliding scale from $10. Same night, bigger room: Emily St. John Mandel at 7:30 with a new novel, $10–128 — the one that will sell out.
