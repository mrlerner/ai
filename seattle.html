NATURE & SKY — THE CAVEAT CAME OFF THIS MORNING
The Harvest Moon night now has a forecast, and it is clear: mostly sunny, 63°, 8% rain, Saturday the 26th
THU SEP 24 – SUN SEP 27  ·  MOONRISE 6:15, 6:30, 6:45, 7:05PM  ·  FREE  ·  ANY EAST-FACING SHORE
Friday and Saturday this brief carried this with an honest hole in it: the 26th sat outside the seven-day forecast, so there was no way to say whether the sky would be clear for it, and Thursday and Friday were offered as backups. The 26th is now inside the file: mostly sunny, high 63°, 8% chance of rain. And the backups have gone the other way — Thursday night is 44% rain and Friday night 36%. So the ranking has inverted. Saturday is no longer just the astronomically correct night. It is the only one of the four with a clear forecast attached.
What actually happens. The equinox is Tuesday at 5:05 in the afternoon; the full moon nearest it gets called the Harvest Moon, and this year that is Saturday September 26, at 99.8% lit. The moon normally rises about fifty minutes later each night. Around this particular full moon its path lies at a shallow angle to our horizon and that gap collapses — computed again this morning for this latitude: Thursday 6:15pm, Friday 6:30, Saturday 6:45, Sunday 7:05. Fifteen to twenty minutes a night instead of fifty. Four evenings running with a nearly full moon coming up around dinner time, which is the entire reason the harvest got named after it.
Saturday is the one. Sunset that evening is 7:00:30. The moon’s upper edge clears the horizon at about 6:45 and the whole disc is up by 6:50 — so the full moon is in the sky for a quarter of an hour before the sun goes down, rising in real daylight rather than into the dark. It comes up at an azimuth of 81 degrees, a shade north of due east, which puts it out of the Cascade foothills rather than behind the Issaquah Alps.
Where to stand: you need an unobstructed eastern horizon over water, which here means the Lake Washington shore — Seward Park, Magnuson Park, Madrona Beach, or the Mount Baker shoreline. Be there by 6:30. It is big and orange for the first fifteen minutes and ordinary after that, and the whole thing is finished by about a quarter past seven, which makes it a rare sky event that fits inside your evening rather than defeating it. Free. No event page, so no image on this card.
TODAY — THREE THINGS END AT ONCE, ON THE LAST WARM SUNDAY
Closing day at Seattle Center, closing day on the boat piers, and the last floating concert of the summer
SUN SEP 20  ·  70° AND CLEAR  ·  TWO OF THE THREE ARE FREE
The coincidence is the point. Three separate things that have been running for days or months all finish today, and today is 70° and cloudless — one of the last two such days this year. Nothing on this list repeats before next summer.
Late morning — Sea Mar Fiestas Patrias, 11am to 6pm, free, all ages, final day. Part of Festál, the year-round run of free cultural festivals on the Seattle Center campus; this one marks the Mexican and Central American independence days. It spreads across the Armory food hall, Fisher Pavilion and the Fisher rooftop, so half of it is under a roof. Today is the quieter of its two days. Practical note from the venue: the International Fountain is closed for maintenance through Friday.
Afternoon — the paddle-out concert on Lake Union, 3 to 6pm, free, and you arrive by boat. Lake Union Concerts moor a stage on a barge off the west shore of Gas Works Park and the audience floats around it. This is the eighth and final show of the 2026 season. Three local disc jockeys — Fame Sounds, N8VBOY, Average Citizens. Two details that keep it on your list: it runs 3 to 6pm, so you are off the water and dry before dinner, and the organisers’ own terms require a Coast Guard-approved life jacket and ban alcohol and intoxicants outright. Registration is free. The Gas Works shoreline put-in is closed for construction — use the Sunnyside Boat Launch, the Fremont Canal, or the Lake Washington Rowing Club dock. To rent instead of haul: Northwest Outdoor Center on west Lake Union, or Agua Verde on Portage Bay. A north wind of two to six makes the crossing flat.
Or, if you would rather look at boats than sit in one — the Boats Afloat Show, 10am to 5pm, final day, $21 online or $24 at the gate. A hundred and thirty-odd vessels in the water at the South Lake Union piers, 901 Fairview Avenue North, running since 1978. Free seminars are included with admission. Sunday closes at five rather than six, so this is the earliest of the three to expire. A listings site prints the price as “$0–24” for this — the zero is for under-thirteens.
ON THE WATER — NEW THIS MORNING, AND IT IS THE LAST ONE OF THE YEAR
Strangers take you sailing on Lake Union for nothing, next Sunday — then not again until April
SUN SEP 27  ·  SIGN UP IN PERSON FROM 1PM  ·  FREE  ·  1010 VALLEY ST, SOUTH LAKE UNION
A correction first, because this brief had it wrong. The source list here has said the Center for Wooden Boats — the working maritime museum at the south end of Lake Union — runs a free public sail every Sunday. It does not. Read off their own page this morning, it is monthly, and the 2026 dates were April 12, May 10, June 14, July 26, August 30 and September 27. That last one is next Sunday, and it is the final one of the season.
How it works. Volunteer skippers and crew take members of the public out on the lake, free of charge, for rides of about forty-five minutes. Four to six boats are usually out — sprit boats, steamboats, electric launches, schooners, ketches, yawls — carrying anywhere from four to twenty passengers each. Sign-ups are in person only, starting at 1pm, in front of the Wagner Education Center; boats depart between 1:15 and 5pm and you are given a departure time when you sign up. They have been doing it for more than twenty-eight years.
Arrive early and know the ceiling. It is first come, first served, and by their own account they have between 80 and 200 seats depending on which boats are seaworthy and which volunteers turn up — they say plainly that they cannot always take everyone who queues. On the last date of the season that is worth a head start.
The bonus, which only applies to this date. The last Sunday of each month is Steamship Sunday, so there will be two lines at 1pm — one for the museum’s own boats, one for the Virginia V, the 1922 steamship that is the last of the Puget Sound Mosquito Fleet still running. Her final ride of the day leaves at 3pm. September 27 is the only date in the rest of the year where both happen together.
Weather: next Sunday is one day past the current file, so no number yet — but Saturday the 26th came in this morning at mostly sunny and 63°, so the weekend is trending settled. Indoor fallback if it turns: the museum’s own exhibits and boat shop are in the same building, and the Museum of History and Industry is a four-minute walk across the park.

IDEAS — TWO ROOMS, ONE BUILDING, SAME 7:30 START
Old-growth Cascadia read through its birds, or a novelist on the ruins of Los Angeles — Town Hall has double-booked itself on equinox night
TUE SEP 22, 7:30PM  ·  FROM $10 EITHER WAY  ·  1119 EIGHTH AVE, FIRST HILL

The nature one — Marina Richie, in conversation with Maria Mudd Ruth, the Wyncote Northwest Forum, $10–35 sliding scale. Her book Feathered Forest: Aloft with Birds in Ancient Trees takes the old-growth forests of this corner of the world and reads them one canopy layer at a time, through the birds that live in each. If you would rather be outside on the last warm evening — Pier 62 on the downtown waterfront runs a free movement-and-wellness hour, 6 to 7pm, out over the water. Done in time to be home well before nine.
THURSDAY — THE FIRST WET EVENING, AND IT IS TRIPLE-BOOKED
Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor on the people betting on collapse — with the Mayor of Seattle moderating
THU SEP 24, 7:30PM  ·  $10–50 SLIDING  ·  TOWN HALL GREAT HALL, FIRST HILL
Thursday is where the week breaks — 63°, a slight chance of rain after five, and 44% overnight. It is the first evening you would want a roof, and there are three things competing for it.
The big one. Klein and Taylor are out on End Times Fascism and the Fight for the Living World, about the politics of people who have decided the future is a write-off and are positioning accordingly. The angle for you is who is holding the microphone: Mayor Katie Wilson is moderating — a sitting mayor conducting a public argument about collapse and cities, which is not a thing that happens often. $10–50 sliding, with an optional book add-on.
The free one — Christopher W. Smith at the Burke Museum, 7pm, doors 6:30, free with registration. Eighteen years of work reconstructing Samuel Elwitt (1834–1919), a Tsimshian master carver of the Kitselas First Nation whose spoons, model poles, bowls and headdresses sit in the British Museum and the American Museum of Natural History — and whom most scholars of Northwest Coast art have never heard of, because of how museums catalogued him. Smith is a museum anthropologist in Vancouver. The Burke is on the University of Washington campus, 4303 Memorial Way Northeast. One caution: their event page is currently displaying a “this event is in the past” banner on a future date — a site glitch, but register early and confirm rather than just turning up.
And the film one: Life in The Sound at the Northwest Film Forum, 7pm, $15 — a soundscape ecologist and her brother on how sound acts on people. Carried over from Friday: despite the title, it is about acoustic ecology, not Puget Sound.
