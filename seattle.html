This week in Seattle
Tomorrow is one of the six days a year they hand out free rides on the wooden fleet
SUN AUG 30 — SIGN-UP 1PM, BOATS 1:15–5PM  ·  FREE  ·  CENTER FOR WOODEN BOATS, 1010 VALLEY ST, SOUTH LAKE UNION
The Center for Wooden Boats is the working maritime museum at the south end of Lake Union, and the Public Sail is the part that is not a museum at all: volunteer skippers take walk-up passengers out on the historic fleet for about forty-five minutes on the water, free. It ran in the brief earlier this week as a thing to plan around; it is now tomorrow, and the forecast held. Sign-ups are in person in front of the Wagner Education Center starting at 1pm sharp — first come, first served, everyone in your party present, no advance booking, no holding a spot. Between eighty and two hundred seats go out depending on how many boats and crews show up. Lifejackets provided, no age limit, digital waiver at sign-up, donations welcome and not expected.
The scarcity is the story: they scheduled it six days in all of 2026 — April 12, May 10, June 14, July 26, tomorrow, and then September 27. Sunday reads 70°, mostly cloudy, 3% rain, with a southeast wind around 5 mph, which is enough air to sail on and not enough to be uncomfortable. A free thing on the first dry day after a thunderstorm Saturday will draw a line, so treat 1pm as the start and not the target. If it is hopeless: the boathouse floats and livery dock next door are free to wander, or take your own boat off the paddle rentals at the Northwest Outdoor Center on the west side of the lake.
The salmon ladder is mid-handoff, and Monday is the last warm evening to stand over it
FISH LADDER OPEN DAILY 7AM–9PM  ·  FREE  ·  CONCERT SUN AUG 30, 2–4PM  ·  BALLARD LOCKS, 3015 NW 54TH ST
Late August is the changing of the guard in the Lake Washington Ship Canal fish ladder: chinook peak in August — roughly fifteen thousand in an average year — and coho peak in September, about twenty-five thousand, so the last days of the month are the one stretch where the underwater viewing windows can show you both. It costs nothing, the room is open 7am to 9pm every day, and the lock chambers cycling boats through are free theatre on the way in. Monday at 73° and mostly sunny is the warmest, driest evening of the next seven days, and the windows stay open till nine — an after-work hour there is the cheapest good use of it in the city. Tomorrow instead, the Carl S. English Jr. Botanical Garden lawn has a free concert, 2 to 4pm, part of a volunteer-run summer series that runs to Labor Day.
Ballard, walkable from the north side, and the whole thing is outdoors except the ladder room and visitor center, which is the built-in rain plan. If you would rather go once and go big, the series closes with three straight afternoons over Labor Day weekend, Saturday through Monday, 2 to 4pm, including a Celtic set on the Sunday. Bring a chair or a blanket — there is no seating and donations pay the performers.
Two free nights in the radio station's own room, timed to an arena show you are not going to
TUE SEP 1 & WED SEP 2, 4:30–8PM  ·  FREE, RESERVE ONLINE  ·  21+  ·  KEXP GATHERING SPACE, 472 1ST AVE N, SEATTLE CENTER
KEXP, the listener-supported station at Seattle Center, opens its street-level Gathering Space on both nights that Tame Impala plays the arena next door, with station disc jockeys running the room and no arena ticket required. It is free, but the reservation link matters — the room is not large and these fill. Both nights are 21 and over.
A 4:30 start and an 8pm finish is about as accommodating as this city's music calendar gets, and it is indoors, which matters on the Wednesday: that day is 65° and rain, 76%, the wettest of the week. Tuesday is the better of the two — 73° and dry until about 5pm, so you can walk the Seattle Center grounds first and be inside before the rain starts.

The natural history museum drops admission and stays open to eight — the only version that works on a weeknight
THU SEP 3, 10AM–8PM  ·  FREE, RESERVE ONLINE  ·  BURKE MUSEUM, 4303 MEMORIAL WAY NE, UNIVERSITY OF WASHINGTON
Washington's museum of natural history and culture waives admission on the first Thursday of the month and pushes closing from five to eight in the evening, which is the difference between a weekend errand and something you can actually do after work. The building is designed inside out: the fossil preparation lab, the collections and the herbarium are all visible through glass while staff work. Up now: Woven in Wool, on the revival of Coast Salish weaving, alongside the permanent fossil and Northwest native cultures halls.
Free but reserve online — first Thursdays run heavy. Thursday reads 65° and rain likely, 58%, so indoors is the honest call anyway, and the light rail to University of Washington station saves you the campus parking problem entirely.
Seven Seattle singers spend a night on the women who were on the radio in 1993
THU SEP 3 — DOORS 6:30PM, MUSIC 7:30PM  ·  $20 ADVANCE / $25 DOOR  ·  THE ROYAL ROOM, 5000 RAINIER AVE S, COLUMBIA CITY
The Royal Room's Great Women of Folk & Country series takes a themed run at a songbook each time; this installment is GenX Radio — Shania Twain, Tracy Chapman, Suzanne Vega and the rest of the voices that came out of a car stereo in that decade. Seven local singers split it: Amy Laybourn, Jean Mann, Kat Lorme, Kristy Smith, Sherri Jerome, Stephanie Robbins and Sue Quigley. The Royal Room is the neighborhood jazz-and-everything room in Columbia City, food served, sight lines from anywhere in the house.
Advance tickets are online only, and the tables are held for people who bought ahead — general seating is first come, first served, so buying the $20 in advance is both the cheaper and the better seat. A 7:30 downbeat in Columbia City on a rainy Thursday is a short, contained evening.
A congressman, a litigator and a congresswoman on the same problem from three ends
FRI SEP 4 — DOORS 6:30PM, TALK 7:30PM  ·  $10–$35 SLIDING SCALE  ·  TOWN HALL SEATTLE, 1119 8TH AVE, FIRST HILL
Congressman Jared Huffman, on his book No Prophets about the separation of church and state, and Skye Perryman, the litigator who runs Democracy Forward, on Ordinary People, Extraordinary Times — moderated by Congresswoman Pramila Jayapal. The legislative end, the courtroom end and the organizing end of one argument, in one room, which is the version of this conversation that is worth leaving the house for.
The sliding scale is real and unpoliced: $10 buys the same seat as $35. It is in the Wyncote Northwest Forum, the downstairs room, entrance off Seneca Street. These run about seventy-five minutes, so you are out before nine.
The Esperanto society has booked the horror film William Shatner made entirely in Esperanto
FRI SEP 4, 7PM — 74 MINUTES, ONE SHOWING  ·  THE BEACON, 4405 RAINIER AVE S, COLUMBIA CITY
One night only, and about as one-off as this city gets: the Seattle Esperanto Society presents Incubus, the 1966 folk-horror picture shot entirely in the constructed language Esperanto, with Shatner a year before Star Trek — the cast learned the lines phonetically in a few weeks and the director banned dubbing. The Beacon's own description is the honest pitch: Ingmar Bergman directing an episode of The Outer Limits. It was shot in Big Sur by Conrad Hall, who went on to three cinematography Oscars, and the print is a restoration from the last known surviving 35mm copy.
Seventy-four minutes at 7pm means out the door by 8:20, which is the rare Friday night film that fits. Straight conflict with the Town Hall panel, same evening, same start — and the Beacon is the small room inside the Columbia City Theater building, four doors from the Royal Room, so a Thursday there and a Friday here is one three-block week.
Volunteers haul telescopes to a Shoreline park and let strangers look through them
SAT SEP 5, 8PM  ·  FREE, RSVP REQUESTED  ·  PARAMOUNT PARK, 15300 8TH AVE NE, SHORELINE
The Seattle Astronomical Society runs free public star parties in area parks, and the next one in town is the Paramount Park night, cleared with Shoreline Parks and Recreation: members bring telescopes and binoculars and put them on whatever the season offers, all experience levels, families welcome. The society's advice is to dress warmly and bring a camping chair, a blanket, something hot and your own binoculars if you have a pair. Their earlier evening is the gentler entry point: Introduction to Night Sky and Telescopes, free, Friday Sep 4 at 7pm in Woodinville, two members walking through basic sky navigation and how a telescope actually works — worth it if you have ever considered buying one.
Honest about the hour: this is an 8pm start that runs late, and Shoreline is twenty minutes up the highway — the first hour is the part worth having. RSVP and they email you if it is scrubbed; a cancellation call goes up by 4pm on the day, which is exactly the right policy for a Seattle sky. The Friday talk is indoors and weatherproof.
Quiet on the tide and aurora feeds. Nothing in the next eight days gets near the −2 ft that makes a tidepooling trip worth the drive — the water only turns negative pre-dawn from Wednesday, −0.4 ft at 3:51am September 3 down to −0.8 ft at 7:05am September 6, the deepest and the only one at a human hour. Geomagnetic activity did touch Kp 5.67, but the peak fell at 2pm today in full daylight and it settles to Kp 2–3 for the rest of the weekend, so there is no aurora to chase. The Aurigid meteor shower peaks the night of August 31 into September 1 at roughly six an hour, into a last-quarter moon — too few and too washed out to set an alarm for, though Monday night is the clear one if you happen to be up.
