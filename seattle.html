Six nights out: the moon clears the horizon already eaten, and it is the only thing this week that cannot be rescheduled
THU AUG 27  ·  MOONRISE 7:51PM, DEEPEST 9:12PM, ENDS 10:52PM  ·  FREE, NAKED EYE  ·  ANY OPEN EAST-SOUTHEAST HORIZON
A deep partial lunar eclipse, and Seattle draws an odd hand on it. The partial phase begins at 7:33pm — eighteen minutes before the moon rises here at 7:51pm — so it comes up already bitten rather than turning while you watch. The sun sets eight minutes after that at 7:59pm and civil twilight ends at 8:31pm, so the sky is draining exactly as the shadow deepens. Maximum is 9:12pm with about 96 percent of the disk inside the umbra: nearly total, most of the face gone coppery with one bright rind left on it. The moon is fully out of the shadow by 10:52pm.
The whole difficulty is altitude — through the good hour the moon is only a few degrees up in the east-southeast, so any treeline or ridge takes it. That points at the Lake Washington shore, which is the one direction in this city with water under an eastern horizon: Madrona, Leschi, Seward Park, or Magnuson looking south down the lake. Golden Gardens and Alki both face exactly the wrong way. Bedtime-compatible version: it is already deeply into shadow by 8:45pm, so the best of it fits before nine. Thursday's daytime forecast is 72° and partly sunny; the seven-day file does not yet reach Thursday night, so the cloud call is still open.
What to do with an 81° Friday evening when every ticketed thing in town starts at eight
TONIGHT AND ALL WEEK  ·  VIEWING ROOM 7AM–9PM  ·  NO ADMISSION CHARGE  ·  HIRAM M. CHITTENDEN LOCKS, BALLARD
Tonight is the warmest evening of the next seven days and the Friday calendar answers it with 8pm doors and a 9pm outdoor movie — both past the hour that works. The unticketed answer is ten minutes from home: the fish ladder is inside the chinook peak right now, and the state's count at the Locks was 545 fish on August 16 against a running season total of 4,934. The underwater windows stay open until 9pm, and the Carl S. English garden above them is a botanical garden that most people walk straight past on the way to the lock gates.
Worth a second trip Sunday: HyperResonance plays the free 2pm concert on the garden lawn, one of the last weekends of a series that ends September 7 — no seating provided, so bring a chair or a blanket. Sunday is 73° and partly sunny at 3 percent rain, the safe half of the weekend. Donations at the concert are voluntary and go to the garden and the education programs.
The park built on a lid over Interstate 5 turns fifty, and the party is free, outdoors and over by three
SAT AUG 22, 12–3PM  ·  FREE, NO TICKET  ·  FREEWAY PARK, 700 SENECA ST, FIRST HILL
Freeway Park opened on July 4, 1976 as the first park in the world built on a deck over a working freeway — Lawrence Halprin's concrete canyons and waterfalls poured directly over the interstate, and the direct ancestor of every lid proposal argued about in this city since. The fiftieth-anniversary celebration runs parkwide with public art, live music and free food, put on jointly by Lid I-5, the Seattle Parks Foundation and the Freeway Park Association, with future-of-the-lid conversations folded in. For someone who writes civic software, this is the one afternoon all year where the people who want to build more of it are all standing in the same three acres.
Weather call, and it is not a good one: Saturday carries a 50 percent chance of rain before 4pm at 72°, which is precisely the noon-to-three window. Halprin built the place in concrete and overhangs, so there is more shelter here than in an open park, but go dressed for it. Indoor fallback four blocks away: the Central Library, whose eleven floors are their own architectural argument, and Town Hall, where the Solstice Symphony Orchestra plays Stravinsky's Firebird and Rachmaninoff's Second at 7:30pm for $12–18.

A twenty-year-old record played end to end in Ballard, on the drier half of the weekend
SUN AUG 23, 7:30PM  ·  $27  ·  21+  ·  TRACTOR TAVERN, BALLARD AVENUE
Horse Feathers — the Portland string band whose thing is fingerpicked guitar under cello and fiddle, quiet enough that a loud room ruins it — marks the twentieth anniversary of “Words Are Dead” by playing it whole, presented by the Bellevue community radio station KBCS. The Tractor holds a few hundred people under wagon wheels on Ballard Avenue and is the right size for a band that plays this softly; a 7:30 Sunday start is also the friendliest slot the room offers all week.
Stacks neatly with the Locks concert earlier the same day — both are in Ballard, twelve minutes apart, on a 73° partly sunny Sunday. Indoors either way, so the forecast is not the deciding factor here.

The natural history museum stages its first fashion show, and it is the last night of the exhibition it closes
MON AUG 24, 6–9PM  ·  $55, $45 MEMBERS, ADVANCE ONLY  ·  BURKE MUSEUM, UNIVERSITY OF WASHINGTON CAMPUS
“Woven in Wool: Resilience in Coast Salish Weaving” closes with seven Coast Salish artists and weavers walking new woven wool work and jewelry — the first fashion show the Burke has ever held — followed by a reception with heavy hors d'oeuvres. The exhibition itself is about a weaving tradition that was nearly severed and has been deliberately rebuilt over the last few decades, so a runway of new pieces rather than a lecture about old ones is the point being made.
This is the priciest thing in the brief and the one with a hard deadline — tickets are advance-purchase, no walk-up, and when the evening ends the show comes down. A 6pm start is the good news; the 9pm end is the outer edge, and the runway portion comes before the reception, so leaving early costs you the food and not the substance. Monday is 75° and mostly sunny with zero rain, which makes the walk down to the Montlake Cut afterward worth building in.

The best outdoor evening of the next seven days is Tuesday, and Lake Union has had something booked on it for fifty-two years
TUE AUG 25, FIRST START 7PM  ·  FREE TO WATCH  ·  LAKE UNION — GAS WORKS PARK, OR ON THE WATER
The Duck Dodge is the Tuesday-night sailboat race that has gone around Lake Union every summer since the early seventies: costumed crews, a fresh theme each week, staggered starts by boat speed from 7pm, and a fleet that is unmistakably out there for the evening rather than the trophy. Tuesday reads 77° and mostly sunny at 1 percent rain — the warmest and cleanest day of the whole rebuild, better than either weekend day — and the season runs out in September.
Two free ways in: the hill on the north shore at Gas Works looks straight down the course, or put a boat in and watch from inside the spectator fleet — the Northwest Outdoor Center and Agua Verde both rent on the lake, both a short paddle from the start. Racing finishes well before dark. Monday at 75° and zero percent is the backup if something takes Tuesday.
A Northwest field guide arranged by season instead of by species — ten dollars if you want it to be, and out by a quarter to nine
WED AUG 26, DOORS 6:30PM, 7:30PM  ·  $10–$35 SLIDING SCALE  ·  THE WYNCOTE NORTHWEST FORUM, TOWN HALL SEATTLE, FIRST HILL
Artist and naturalist Emily Poole launches “Cascade of Life: An Artist's Seasonal Guide to the Wild Northwest,” joined by Kate Rogers, editor-in-chief of the nonprofit Seattle publisher Mountaineers Books, and Katie Campbell, digital editor and reporter at the public radio station KUOW. The book walks shoreline to alpine to shrub-steppe a season at a time, illustration beside essay, and the evening doubles as a preview of the publisher's whole fall natural-history list.
Town Hall runs these at roughly seventy-five minutes, so 7:30 means out around 8:45 — a lecture that fits the bedtime with nothing given up. The sliding scale starts at ten dollars and is not means-tested. Wednesday is 72° with rain chances only at 18 percent, so nothing outdoors is being sacrificed for it.
