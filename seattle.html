NATURE & SKY — THE FIRST THING THIS FEED HAS PRODUCED IN TWELVE DAYS
A minor geomagnetic storm is forecast for tomorrow night — and a 93% moon is going to sit in the sky the entire time
WED SEP 23, 8:49PM–MIDNIGHT  ·  FREE  ·  NORTH-FACING SHORELINE, AWAY FROM CITY LIGHT
The numbers, straight off the Space Weather Prediction Center’s three-day forecast issued at 12:30 this afternoon Greenwich time. The planetary Kp index — the standard measure of how disturbed the Earth’s magnetic field is — is forecast at 4.33 between five and eight tomorrow evening, 5.33 between eight and eleven, and 4.67 from eleven until two in the morning. That 5.33 is a G1, the lowest rung of the storm scale, and it is the first reading above Kp 4 this brief has seen since it started watching. The cause is named in their own rationale: a coronal hole high-speed stream, a gap in the sun’s corona letting fast solar wind out towards us. Kp 5 is the threshold at which the aurora becomes possible low on the northern horizon at this latitude — not overhead, not in colour to the naked eye, a pale glow above the north hills that a camera will see better than you will.
Now the three things working against it, because I would rather you knew before driving anywhere. One: the moon. Recomputed this morning from the Jet Propulsion Laboratory’s ephemeris — tomorrow night the moon is 93% lit and above the horizon the whole window, climbing from 17° at eight o’clock to 31° at half past eleven, and it does not set until about 4:35am. That is a lot of light in the sky. Two: cloud. The gridded sky cover for downtown is beautiful early — 18% at five, six and seven — and then goes to 54% at eight and stays there, which is exactly when you need it. Rain chance is zero all night, so this is cloud, not weather. Three: darkness arrives late. Astronomical twilight does not end until 8:49pm, so the first hour of the Kp peak is still in dusk.
The one piece of good news in the geometry. The moon spends the evening in the south and southwest — azimuth 132° at eight, 194° at midnight. The aurora, if it shows, is due north. So you are not staring into the moon; you are turning your back on it. That matters more than the raw illumination figure suggests.
What I would actually do. Treat it as twenty minutes, not an expedition. Golden Gardens in Ballard, Carkeek Park, Discovery Park’s north bluff, or the Edmonds waterfront — anywhere with open water and open sky to the north and the city glow behind you. Go out between nine and half past ten, look north, give your eyes ten minutes to adjust, and take a long-exposure photograph even if you see nothing, because the camera is the honest witness at Kp 5. If it is solidly overcast when you step outside, do not chase it — a G1 is a nice-to-have, not a once-a-decade event, and the same coronal hole tends to come back around in about twenty-seven days.
TONIGHT — AND YOU ARE HOME BY NINE
Old-growth Cascadia climbed one canopy layer at a time, with the birds of each level as the guide
TUE SEP 22, 7:30PM (OUT BY ~8:45)  ·  $10–35 SLIDING  ·  WYNCOTE NORTHWEST FORUM, FIRST HILL
Marina Richie, in conversation with the natural history writer Maria Mudd Ruth. Her new book Feathered Forest: Aloft with Birds in Ancient Trees starts at her home in central Oregon and works outward across both sides of the Cascades, through forests her publisher is willing to compare to Amazonian rainforest for sheer accumulated biological complexity. Same building, same start time, the other one: Emily St. John Mandel in the Great Hall downstairs, $10–128, on Exit Party — a man vanishes from a party in a Los Angeles that is one spring past the collapse of the United States. Both have a $10 tier, so it is a choice rather than a budget decision.
And the free one, if you want to stay off First Hill: the sports writer Dave Zirin with the Seattle teacher and writer Jesse Hagopian at the Elliott Bay Book Company on Capitol Hill, 7 to 8:30pm, free with a reservation. THURSDAY — THE WETTEST NIGHT, AND THE BEST ROOM TO BE IN
Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor on the people betting on collapse — with the Mayor of Seattle asking the questions
THU SEP 24, 7:30PM (OUT BY ~8:45)  ·  $10–50 SLIDING  ·  TOWN HALL GREAT HALL, FIRST HILL
Thursday deteriorated for a second consecutive morning — 59% rain yesterday, 68% today, 87% overnight, and 88 to 93% cloud cover from five o’clock until midnight. There is no version of Thursday evening that happens outdoors.
Which is convenient, because this is the best ticket of the week. Klein and Taylor are out on End Times Fascism and the Fight for the Living World, arguing that religious fundamentalists, Silicon Valley technologists and ethno-nationalists have converged on one shared conviction — that a cleansing catastrophe is coming and they will be on the right side of it — and that believing this makes you welcome the fire instead of fighting it. The private island, the rocket to Mars, the bunkered nation: three versions of the same bet. The reason to go rather than read it is who is holding the microphone. Mayor Katie Wilson is moderating — a sitting mayor running a public argument about collapse and who gets to survive it, in her own city. $10–50 sliding, optional book add-on, doors 6:30, finished by about 8:45.
The free alternative, same night, and it is a real one: Christopher W. Smith at the Burke Museum on the University of Washington campus, 7pm, doors 6:30, free with registration — eighteen years of work reconstructing Samuel Elwitt (1834–1919), a Tsimshian master carver of the Kitselas First Nation whose bowls and headdresses sit unattributed in the British Museum and the American Museum of Natural History. Their page has been showing a “this event is in the past” banner on a future date for several days now. It is a site fault, not a cancellation, but register ahead rather than turning up.
And if you would rather sit in the dark: Life in The Sound at the Northwest Film Forum on Capitol Hill, 7pm, $15 general and $10 for students, children and seniors — a year with a soundscape ecologist and her brother studying what sound does to people. Part of the Local Sightings festival of Pacific Northwest film, which is in its closing week.

SATURDAY — THE FULL MOON, AND THE FORECAST HAS SWUNG BACK IN ITS FAVOUR
The Harvest Moon clears the horizon ten minutes before the sun sets — and this morning Saturday’s sky is the clearer one again
SAT SEP 26, MOONRISE 6:50PM  ·  SUNSET 7:00:30PM  ·  FREE, FROM ANY EAST-FACING SHORE
Recomputed this morning rather than copied forward — Jet Propulsion Laboratory ephemeris, refracted, five-minute steps for downtown Seattle. Saturday is the full moon: 99.76% lit at midnight, against 99.52% on Sunday, which makes it the Harvest Moon, the full moon nearest the equinox. Moonrise is 6:50pm at azimuth 81°, a shade north of due east; sunset is 7:00:30pm. The moon is up and clear of the horizon ten minutes before the sun goes down — which is the entire reason to bother, because you get it large and orange against a sky that is still lit.
The Harvest Moon effect, in the numbers that produce it. Moonrise runs Thursday 6:15pm, Friday 6:30, Saturday 6:50, Sunday 7:05, Monday 7:30 — gaps of fifteen, twenty, fifteen and twenty-five minutes against a usual fifty. That near-standstill is the phenomenon itself, and it means four consecutive evenings deliver a nearly full moon at a civilised hour. Thursday and Friday are written off by cloud; Saturday and Sunday are the usable pair.
And the forecast has moved back. Yesterday I corrected myself in this space to say Sunday’s evening sky was the clearer of the two — 48% against Saturday’s 56%. This morning’s gridded numbers for five to nine in the evening are Saturday 42% cloud with 2% rain, Sunday 64% cloud with 20% rain. Saturday is now clearly the better night, and it is the night of the actual full moon, so the two agree for once. The honest read is that both figures have moved about fifteen points in two days and neither is settled — but if you only go out once, go out Saturday.
Where to stand, and what else the day is good for. You want an unobstructed eastern horizon across water: Seward Park, Magnuson, Madrona Beach, Mount Baker Beach. Be in place by 6:30 — the ten minutes either side of moonrise are the whole show. Saturday is also the best daylight of the week after Wednesday: mostly sunny, 63°, 4% rain. It is the day to take the boat out, walk a beach, or drive to larches if you are minded to. Free, and it wants nothing but a coat.
SUNDAY — THE LAST FREE BOAT RIDE UNTIL APRIL
Volunteers give away rides on Lake Union in wooden boats, six times a year, and this is the sixth
SUN SEP 27  ·  FREE  ·  SIGN-UP IN PERSON FROM 1PM, 1010 VALLEY ST, SOUTH LAKE UNION
The Center for Wooden Boats runs its free public sail monthly, not weekly — re-read off their page this morning, and the 2026 list is six dates long: April 12, May 10, June 14, July 26, August 30, and Sunday. Then nothing until spring. Volunteer skippers take passengers out in sprit boats, schooners, ketches, yawls, and steam and electric launches; rides are about 45 minutes, boats depart between 1:15 and 5pm, four to six boats, 80 to 200 seats in total.
The catch, in their own words: sign-ups are in person only, from 1pm, in front of the Wagner Education Center, first come first served, and they state plainly that they cannot always take everyone who queues. Arrive before one.
The bonus that only lands on this date. Steamship Sunday is the last Sunday of every month, so it coincides: a second queue, also from 1pm, for the Virginia V, the 1922 steamship, last ride at 3pm. Two lines on the same dock. Weather: 62°, cloudy, 20% rain and only after five — fine for an afternoon on the water even though the evening has gone grey.
And the same evening, if you want a roof afterwards: closing night of Local Sightings, the Northwest Film Forum’s twenty-ninth annual festival of Pacific Northwest film — The Joyless Economy, 7pm, Capitol Hill. Ten days of regional work ends there.
