This week in Seattle
Water & nature · free · the run broke open
1,774 coho went up the Ballard Locks ladder in one day — the season nearly doubled in a single count, and the peak is still ahead
SEASON TOTAL 4,089 THROUGH SEP 10  ·  FREE, OPEN DAILY  ·  HIRAM M. CHITTENDEN LOCKS, BALLARD
This brief has been tracking a run that was creeping — 13 fish one day, 238 another. Then the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife posted September 10: 1,774 coho in a single day, more than every other day of the season put together. The season total goes 2,315 → 4,089. Chinook had a big day too, 727 on the 10th, 13,121 for the season. Sockeye finished back in July at 31,302 and has not moved since.
Why this is the week and not next: a jump that size usually means the bulk of the run has arrived at the door rather than that it is over — the coho peak at the locks is the last two weeks of September, so this is the front edge of it. The counts are marked preliminary and post in clumps, so the true number today is already higher than the page says. The underwater viewing gallery at the fish ladder puts you at glass level with the fish as they hold against the current between steps of the ladder; it costs nothing, it is open daily, and twenty minutes does it properly.
Weather call: not tonight and not Sunday morning. Wednesday through Friday run 73°, 75° and 76° at 1%, 0% and 1% — the warmest, driest stretch in the eight-day file, and the last of it is the warmest day. The locks, the botanical garden beside them and the Ballard side of the ship canal is a comfortable ninety minutes. The indoor fallback is the attraction itself: the viewing gallery is below ground and dry, so bad weather only spoils the walk in.
Nature & sky · free · forecast reversal
The clouds have taken the moon-and-Venus nights back — the conjunction is Sunday and Monday, the clear sky is Tuesday through Thursday, and they are not the same nights
SUN–THU, THE 20 MINUTES AFTER SUNSET  ·  FREE  ·  ANY OPEN WESTERN HORIZON
The honest update: this brief spent three mornings building toward Sunday and Monday evening, on a forecast of 5% and 0% cloud. Today's hourly sky-cover grid says Sunday at 7pm is 60% cloud and Monday goes 50% at 7pm to 61% by 8. Sunday does thin to 41% — at 8pm, five minutes after Venus has already set. Both nights are now a coin toss you have to drive to.
What is actually happening, computed this morning from the Jet Propulsion Laboratory's ephemeris service for this latitude and longitude. Sunday, 7:30pm (sunset 7:27): Venus 6.1° above the west-southwest horizon at magnitude −4.75, with a 9.8% crescent moon 4.8° to its lower right. Monday, 7:30pm (sunset 7:25): the moon has crossed to the other side — a 16.7% crescent 7.3° to Venus's upper left, and this time the moon is the higher of the two, so it lingers after Venus drops. Both nights Venus is on the rooftops by 7:50 and gone by 8:05.
The trade to make: Tuesday is 2% cloud, Wednesday 0%, Thursday 3% — genuinely clear, the first such evenings in a fortnight. By Tuesday the moon has walked 19° away from Venus, so you lose the tight pairing, but you gain a 24.7% crescent riding higher in a dark sky and a Venus you can actually count on seeing. If you want the conjunction, Monday is still the better of the two bad nights. If you want to be sure of seeing something, go Tuesday or Wednesday.
Where to stand: it all happens low, so it needs water — Golden Gardens, Carkeek, Alki, or Discovery Park's Sound side. Fifteen minutes out there and you are home well before nine. No indoor fallback, only the next clear evening — and Venus is sinking sunward from here, reaching its brightest of the year on the 22nd and getting harder every week after.

Film · $14 · new this morning
Five nights of Nordic film in Ballard, every one of them starting at six and finished by half past eight
TUE–SAT SEP 15–19  ·  $14 A FILM  ·  OSBERG HALL, NATIONAL NORDIC MUSEUM, 2655 NW MARKET ST
The Nordic museum's own festival, five nights in its auditorium ten minutes from home, and this year's programme is queer stories from across the Nordic countries. The shape of it suits you almost suspiciously well: a short at 6:00, the feature at 6:15, out by 8 or 8:30, every single night.
The nights, in order: Tue — Show Me Love (Sweden, 1998), the small-town teenage one that made Lukas Moodysson's name, 6:15–7:45. Wed — Sauna (Denmark, 2025), brand new, 6:15–8:00. Thu — Heartstone (Iceland, 2016), two boys and a fishing village, 6:15–8:25. Fri — And Then We Danced (Sweden/Georgia), with a 6pm pre-film discussion featuring the Georgian art researcher Nikoloz Nadirashvili and the choreographer Joseph Hernandez. Sat is the full day: a 1924 Danish silent at 11am introduced by a University of Washington scholar, then three more through to a 7:30 Norwegian closer.
How to get in: $14 general, $12 members, per film; a Saturday pass is $52 and the full festival $100, which also covers museum admission. Wednesday is the pick — it is the one night this week with nothing else competing for it, and if you would rather have the 0%-cloud sky that evening, the film ends at 8 and Venus has already set, so take the sky Tuesday instead. Entirely indoors either way.

THU SEP 17, 5PM FREE + 7:30PM $10–35  ·  U DISTRICT, THEN FIRST HILL

Confirmed on the university's public-lectures page this morning — and it is the only public lecture the university is putting on before October 8, so this is the whole autumn offering until then. 7:30pm, $10–35 sliding: Gerard Ryle, who directs the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists — the outfit that organised several hundred reporters across eighty countries to break the Panama Papers, won the 2017 Pulitzer for it, and has had roughly $1.4 billion in unpaid tax recovered in its wake. The two fit together better than a coincidence deserves — one is about persuading people without cheating, the other about what it takes to make a fact land when a great deal of money would rather it did not.
