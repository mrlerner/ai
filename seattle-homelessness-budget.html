What Does Seattle Spend on Homelessness?

Updated October 31, 2026. Figures are the 2026 adopted budget unless marked otherwise.

The short answer: the city budgets $229 million for homelessness in 2026, and almost all of it is local tax money. County, state and federal dollars come mostly through a separate regional agency, and Seattle pays for most of that agency too.

There is no single homelessness budget. The money sits in three layers, and they overlap.

Layer 1$228.7M

City of Seattle

The city's official homelessness figure: shelter and service contracts, encampment response and staff. This is the number the City Council controls.

Layer 2$202.9M

King County Regional Homelessness Authority (KCRHA)

A regional pool for all of King County that combines city, county, state and federal money. Seattle provides $117.6M of it, and that money also counts in Layer 1.

Layer 3Not totaled

Everything else

Federal grants paid straight to providers, the county's supportive-housing sales tax, the city's affordable-housing budget, Medicaid and housing vouchers. Real money, but nobody adds it up.

Most of the city's $229 million goes through one department, and most of that is passed on to the regional agency.

Citywide homelessness budget$228.7M

The $228.7M citywide total and the $172.4M HSD figure come from the 2026 Adopted Budget. The Unified Care Team amounts come from Council provisos on $29.9M across seven departments. The ~$32M remainder is the difference between the citywide total and what can be itemized.

The Human Services Department's homelessness budget rose 24% from 2025 ($138.9M → $172.4M). It includes $11.5M for prevention and rental assistance, $20M set aside in case federal funding is cut, and $7.8M to open 155 new shelter units.

County, state and federal money shows up mainly in the regional agency, and much of the county's share is really state money.

The city's own $229M is almost entirely local: the General Fund, a short-term rental tax and the Housing Levy. Of the $126.8M it sends to KCRHA, only $3.9M (about 3%) is federal grant money.

KCRHA's funders look different depending on whether you ask who sends the check or who raised the money, because King County passes state money through.

Funder totals are from KCRHA's 2026 budget presented to its governing board in February 2026 and sum exactly to $202.9M. The county's breakdown uses its adopted figures, which differ slightly from its contract total. "Who raised the money" traces pass-through grants back to their source and is approximate; KCRHA's own footnote puts the mix at 17% federal, 23% state and 60% local.

What KCRHA buys: enhanced shelter (45%), permanent supportive housing (12%), rapid rehousing (12%), tiny-home villages (9%), its own admin and operations (7%), transitional housing (4%), and smaller programs such as day centers, diversion and safe parking. KCRHA spends across the whole county, so not all of this lands in Seattle.

Several big pots of public money aren't counted in either budget.

~$40M a year

Federal HUD grants paid straight to providers

The region gets $64–67M a year from HUD's Continuum of Care program. KCRHA holds $25M; the rest goes straight to providers and housing authorities, mostly for supportive housing for about 4,500 households. This is the money HUD tried to cut in 2025. King County sued and won in June 2026.

0.1% sales tax

King County Health Through Housing

A countywide sales tax that buys and runs supportive housing: 15+ buildings and 1,000+ residents, several of them in Seattle. The county shows about $180M of 2026 spending, but that includes building purchases and bond payments. The yearly operating cost and Seattle's share aren't published.

$349.5M

Seattle Office of Housing

Affordable housing in general, from the Housing Levy, the JumpStart payroll tax and federal grants. That includes building and running about 5,700 supportive homes, many for formerly homeless people. The city doesn't count it as homelessness spending.

Not quantified

Health care and housing vouchers

Medicaid housing supports, the county's behavioral-health levies, and Seattle Housing Authority vouchers for homeless people and veterans. These are large but aren't reported as homelessness spending.

Does this include philanthropy?

No. Every figure on this page is public money. KCRHA's budget shows just $150K in donations. Most private giving goes straight to nonprofits such as Union Gospel Mission and DESC, and nobody tracks it in one place.

Next year the city takes its contracts back from the regional agency and starts paying for them with the JumpStart payroll tax.

  • About $113M in contracts moves back into the city. That's 117 contracts with 40 providers, plus 23 new city staff to manage them. Shelter accounts for $80M of it.
  • JumpStart pays for homelessness services for the first time. The payroll tax was meant for affordable housing; the city is using it to help close a $175M General Fund gap.

How to say it without double counting

  1. Start with the city's $229M. That's what Seattle taxpayers fund through the city budget, and what the City Council decides.
  2. Add the roughly $85M of KCRHA money that doesn't come from Seattle (county, state, federal and other cities), and say it's spread across the whole county.
  3. Then point to the rest as more money that nobody adds up: direct federal grants, the county's housing sales tax, the city's affordable-housing budget and health care.

Don't add KCRHA's $202.9M to the city's $228.7M. That counts Seattle's $117.6M twice.
