Seattle homelessness · first principles

What does Seattle spend on homelessness?

The city budgets $229 million for homelessness in 2026. Almost all of it is local tax money. County, state and federal dollars come mostly through a separate regional agency, and that agency is mostly funded by Seattle too.

The money comes in three layers

There is no single "homelessness budget." Public money for homelessness in Seattle sits in three places, and they overlap, so you can't simply add up the totals.

Layer 1$228.7M

City of Seattle

The city's own official homelessness figure. It covers shelter and service contracts, encampment response and staff. This is the number the City Council controls.

Layer 2$202.9M

King County Regional Homelessness Authority (KCRHA)

A regional pool covering all of King County. It combines city, county, state and federal money. Seattle provides $117.6M of it, which also counts in Layer 1.

Layer 3Not totaled

Everything else

Federal grants paid straight to providers, the county's supportive-housing sales tax, the city's affordable-housing budget, Medicaid and housing vouchers. These are real money, but nobody adds them up.

Where the city's $229 million goes

About three-quarters runs through the Human Services Department (HSD), and most of that is passed on to the regional agency. The rest is spread across other city departments.

HSD's homelessness budget rose 24% from 2025 ($138.9M → $172.4M). It includes $11.5M for prevention and rental assistance, $20M set aside in case federal funding is cut, and $7.8M to open 155 new shelter units.

Where the money comes from

The city's own $229M is almost entirely local: the General Fund, a short-term rental tax and the Housing Levy. Of the $126.8M it sends to the regional agency, only $3.9M (about 3%) is federal grant money.

County, state and federal money shows up mainly in the regional agency, KCRHA. Its funders look different depending on whether you ask who sends the check or who raised the money, because King County's contribution is mostly state money passing through.

The county's breakdown uses its adopted figures, which differ slightly from its contract total. KCRHA's own footnote puts the mix at 17% federal, 23% state and 60% local.

What KCRHA buys: enhanced shelter (45%), permanent supportive housing (12%), rapid rehousing (12%), tiny-home villages (9%), its own admin and operations (7%), transitional housing (4%), and smaller programs such as day centers, diversion and safe parking. KCRHA spends countywide, so not all of this lands in Seattle.

Money nobody adds up

These public dollars also serve homeless people in Seattle, but neither the city's budget nor KCRHA's counts them. Several are big.

~$40M a year

Federal HUD grants paid straight to providers

The region gets $64–67M a year from HUD's Continuum of Care program. KCRHA holds $25M; the rest goes straight to providers and housing authorities, mostly for supportive housing for about 4,500 households. This is the money HUD tried to cut in 2025. King County sued and won in June 2026.

0.1% sales tax

King County Health Through Housing

A countywide sales tax that buys and runs supportive housing: 15+ buildings and 1,000+ residents, several of them in Seattle. The county shows about $180M of 2026 spending, but that includes building purchases and bond payments. The yearly operating cost and Seattle's share aren't published.

$349.5M

Seattle Office of Housing

Affordable housing in general, from the Housing Levy, the JumpStart payroll tax and federal grants. That includes building and running about 5,700 supportive homes, many for formerly homeless people. The city doesn't count it as homelessness spending.

Not quantified

Health care and housing vouchers

Medicaid housing supports, the county's behavioral-health levies, and Seattle Housing Authority vouchers for homeless people and veterans. These are large but aren't reported as homelessness spending.

Does this include philanthropy? No. Every figure on this page is public money. KCRHA's budget shows just $150K in donations. Most private giving goes straight to nonprofits such as Union Gospel Mission and DESC, and nobody tracks it in one place.

What changes in 2027

Mayor Wilson's first budget, proposed in September 2026, is before the City Council now. Here's what it would change for the Human Services Department's homelessness spending.

  • The city takes its contracts back from KCRHA. About $113M in contracts (117 contracts with 40 providers) moves back into HSD, along with 23 new city staff to manage them. Shelter accounts for $80M of it.
  • JumpStart pays for homelessness services for the first time. The payroll tax was meant for affordable housing; the city is using it to help close a $175M General Fund gap.
  • New money: $37M for about 1,000 new shelter beds by the end of 2027, $6.5M more for prevention, and $10.2M for provider inflation.

How to say it without double counting

  1. Start with the city's $229M. That's what Seattle taxpayers fund through the city budget, and what the City Council decides.
  2. Add the roughly $85M of KCRHA money that doesn't come from Seattle (county, state, federal and other cities), noting it's spread across the whole county.
  3. Then point to the rest as "more, but not added up anywhere": direct federal grants, the county's housing sales tax, the city's affordable-housing budget and health care.

Don't add KCRHA's $202.9M to the city's $228.7M. That counts Seattle's $117.6M twice.
